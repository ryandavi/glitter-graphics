History

Glitter Graphics are animated graphics characterized by looping sparkle and glitter effects applied to text, illustrations, or photographs.

As maximalist[1] digital vernacular art, glitter graphics sit comfortably inside both camp[2] and kitsch[3]: excessive, sincere, decorative, and eventually very far from the minimalism that took over mainstream web design. Their roots go back to the personal-website graphics culture of the 1990s and early 2000s, and surviving examples turn up on amateur pages by the early-to-mid 2000s. But the format most people picture when they hear "glitter graphic" belongs to the customizable social-network profiles of the mid-to-late 2000s, MySpace above all, and Blingee, the tool most closely associated with it, arrived in that later stage rather than the original GeoCities-era personal web.

Origins and Early Development

There doesn't seem to be a single identifiable first Glitter Graphic, at least not one I could find. The creator of the familiar 61×29 glitter asset that was copied and reused across countless graphics is also unknown. By the early-to-mid 2000s, glitter graphics were circulating through online forum signatures and turning up here and there on the personal-site platforms that had already given millions of people somewhere to publish their own pages: GeoCities[4], AOL Hometown[5], Tripod[6], Angelfire[7], and Homestead[8]. These hosts weren't primarily known for glitter graphics — GeoCities in particular already had a fully formed visual culture years before glitter showed up on it — but people did make glitter graphics, collect them from other creators, repost them, and write tutorials showing other people how to make their own, and some of that activity happened on these same personal-site hosts.

One surviving example sits on a GeoCities page in the Heartland/Prairie neighborhood, last updated in 2004, that net artist and web historian Olia Lialina came across while cataloguing the platform's visual conventions. She was struck by how out of place it felt against her own sense of GeoCities' timeline: "Glitter is Myspace, not Geocities. Geocities are 90es, not 00s."[89] That doesn't make this the first glitter graphic — nobody knows what that was — but it does put one on GeoCities, on a fully mature page, years before Blingee existed, and it comes from someone who knew that page's visual culture as well as almost anyone. Even she read glitter as belonging to a different, later internet.

The style was applied to almost anything: Disney and cartoon characters, celebrities and bands, dolls, anime, flowers, butterflies, angels, zodiac signs, fantasy art, photographs, holiday graphics, romantic messages, and a lot of hearts. Glitter graphics were also strongly associated with feminine web culture, particularly the highly decorated personal pages, profiles, dolls, friendship graphics, and romantic imagery common on sites used by girls and young women. At the same time, they appeared in emo and scene[9], goth imagery, and what would later be described as McBling[10]. Many early examples share the same basic construction: a short sparkle animation, often reused in different colors, laid over text or an existing image. They served plenty of specific jobs too: a name or username spelled out in glitter, a "thanks for the add" graphic for a new MySpace friend, a birthday or holiday greeting, a romantic message, a comment left on someone else's profile, a forum signature, a profile picture, a personalized photo. Names in particular carry the format back to older sig-tag and custom-graphic culture — asking someone to make you a glitter name graphic wasn't so different from asking a forum designer for a sig tag.

The move from personal homepages to larger online communities did not immediately end page customization. Many still gave users considerable control over backgrounds, fonts, images, and layouts through HTML, CSS, or built-in customization tools. Glitter graphics moved with them. They appeared throughout Neopets[11] petpages, userlookups, and guilds, as well as on Xanga blogs and Piczo pages, and later across Gaia Online[12] and MySpace[13] profiles.

The Personal Web Aesthetic

Most of what follows in this section predates glitter graphics, or developed alongside them without depending on them. It's here anyway because it's the visual and social culture glitter graphics grew out of, and that Blingee later drew on more directly. Net artist Olia Lialina documented this world while it was still the ordinary way to build a website, describing a bright, cluttered, homemade "vernacular web" assembled by amateurs out of animated GIFs, tiled backgrounds, MIDI files, construction signs, and whatever else was lying around.[90] The personal web had already normalized treating a page as something built from separate pieces: animated GIFs, tiled backgrounds, decorative text, websets, buttons and badges, dolls and adoptables, graphics collected from other sites, copied HTML snippets, layouts shared as resources, credit and link-back systems, and a collection built up one found or gifted graphic at a time. None of that meant a piece was free to edit or redistribute however you liked — the Linkware section below covers how possessive some of that culture actually was — but it did teach a generation of web users to treat digital graphics as material: things you could find, save, collect, edit, arrange, credit, trade, and recombine. Blingee later gave that behavior a purpose-built interface.

The look ranged all over the place, but maximalist chaos was never the default. Plenty of people had real taste and a genuine eye for design, and a personal site was often where that eye got trained, one bad color choice and one too many blinking GIFs at a time. Maximalism was always available, though, and nobody was there to stop you: tiled backgrounds fighting five different fonts, animated glitter piled on more animated glitter, a MIDI file autoplaying whether you asked for it or not. Why wouldn't you post every pretty thing you could find? Personal websites weren't designed by a studio or approved by committee, so a site could be beautifully put together, a complete mess, or both at once depending on which page you clicked into.

Most of the time, you had no idea who was visiting your website. There was something slightly voyeuristic about it: someone could find it through a search engine, a webring, another person's links page, or by clicking around for hours, read everything you had written, and leave without saying a word. Visitor-tracking services such as NedStat, eXTReMe Tracking, and later StatCounter could tell you how many visits you were getting, where people had come from, and sometimes what country or region they were in, but the visitors themselves were still mostly anonymous.

Hit counters made at least one part of that audience visible. A little number at the bottom of the page told you roughly how many times the site had been loaded, and because the counter was usually public, it told everybody else too. A high number could make a site look established or popular in much the same way a follower count does now. Guestbook entries, topsite rankings, and banner clicks offered a few more signs that somebody was actually out there, but none of them told you exactly who was reading.

Search engines increasingly influenced which sites were easiest to find, and Google in particular rewarded pages that other sites linked to. But there was no single feed deciding what everyone saw. People found personal sites through search engines, links pages, directories, webrings, topsites, forums, recommendations, and simply following links from one site to another. There was no platform template forcing every page into the same shape either. One person decided, in Notepad, a WYSIWYG editor, or whatever HTML box their host provided, what their little corner of the internet should look like. Some even wrote a whole "why I made this site" manifesto, sometimes running to genuine essay length, about something they'd built simply because they wanted to. I did it for this site!

Building a Personal Website

GeoCities was the best-known and one of the largest free homepage hosts, but Tripod, Angelfire, Homestead, AOL Hometown, and countless smaller services did the same basic job. Its reputation has also been reinforced by preservation: when Yahoo shut GeoCities down in 2009, outside archivists saved an unusually large amount of it, so far more of its old pages remain available to browse than those of many competing hosts. "GeoCities" eventually became shorthand for a whole style of amateur web design: personal sites assembled from whatever graphics, code, and free resources their owner could scrounge up. GeoCities itself leaned hard into the idea of a website as a place of your own. Its users were homesteaders, and early sites lived at numbered addresses inside themed neighborhoods such as Hollywood, Area51, Tokyo, and WestHollywood.[14][15]

A common professional workflow was to design for an 800 × 600 screen in Photoshop, slice the page apart in ImageReady or Fireworks, then rebuild it with HTML tables.[16][17] Teen and hobbyist designers picked up the same tricks secondhand. By the early 2000s, premade table, iframe, and DIV layouts circulated freely, often built around one large image with a tiny scrolling content area dropped into it. Navigation might use an image map or rollover links laid over the artwork. Splash pages asking you to "Enter," microscopic text, custom scrollbars, and layouts built around one photo or illustration became staples of design-heavy personal sites. People kept whole collections of layouts to download, edit, and reuse.[18]

Within the more design-heavy personal-domain scene, especially among younger designers, the look shifted again around 2002. Iframes and image maps became more common, text got even tinier, and a three-part navigation structure appeared on many sites: me / you / www, roughly dividing the site into pages about the owner, pages for visitors, and links to the rest of the web. Navigation became more deliberately organized than the wander-and-discover pages of a few years earlier, which felt cleaner at the time even if some of it has aged terribly. Layouts changed constantly; some people redesigned their entire site every few weeks because they'd learned a new technique or found a style they liked better. Paid domains also developed their own little hosting culture. A domain owner might take someone on as a hostee, giving them a subdomain and space for their own site. Having a good .com that other people wanted attached to their name could become a small status symbol of its own.

Tiny graphics made specifically for websites included tiled backgrounds, animated dividers, bullets, arrows, clipart, pixel sprites, matching Home / About / Links / Guestbook buttons, hit counters, email graphics, welcome signs, Online / Away / Updated graphics, and flashing "Under Construction" banners. Little "NEW!" icons pointed at whatever had changed. Background MIDI, then WAVs and MP3s, made the page audible too. "Best viewed in Netscape," "Best viewed at 800 × 600," browser badges, HTML-validation buttons, "Made with Notepad" graphics, and dated update logs were familiar fixtures.[19][20][21] Then came the gimmicks: marquees, cursor trails, clocks, status-bar messages, disabled right-clicks, rollover images, pop-ups, splash screens, browser-specific entrances. Personal sites rarely felt finished because people kept adding things to them, which probably explains why "Under Construction" survived for so long.

Code moved around almost as freely as the graphics. Funky Chickens HTML Help[22], created in 1999, broke HTML into little effects you could copy straight into your page. Lissa Explains it All was an even earlier pioneer: a colorful, easy-to-follow HTML tutorial site launched in 1997 by 11-year-old Melissa "Lissa" Brown specifically to help kids and beginners build webpages without an automatic page builder. Her notes began on AOL, moved to GeoCities as the site grew, and then moved to their own domain when she was 12. CYP2 / ColorYourProfyle[23] did something similar for AOL profiles and webpages: color tables, moving and fading text, ALT characters, Wingdings and Webdings, font codes, premade profiles, webpage dolls, and whatever else could be squeezed into AOL's tiny editable boxes. You didn't necessarily sit down to "learn web development." You learned enough HTML to change the background, copied a marquee from somewhere, found a color code somewhere else, broke the page, and eventually figured out why. A lot of that learning was just right-clicking someone's page and hitting "View Source" to see how they'd done something, then lifting the part you needed. That's also the actual reason all those disabled-right-click scripts existed: not a mystery gimmick, just an attempt to stop exactly that. It didn't always work, either as a deterrent or as a lock. At seven years old, I copied code straight off a site that hadn't bothered with one, and still got called out for it in my own guestbook.

Some platforms encouraged this directly. Every Neopet came with its own species-specific petpage, a complete little website whose existing HTML could be edited, rearranged, decorated, or replaced entirely. Neopets also kept a Fun Images[24] library of animated graphics with the HTML already written underneath, ready to copy and paste into petpages, userlookups, shops, and guild pages. It was a useful way to learn: you did not always have to start with a blank page or understand the code before touching it. You could begin with something that already worked, change it, break it, fix it, and gradually figure out what everything did.

Some pages existed for one specific obsession. A character shrine could be a single dedicated page within a larger personal site, or an entire mini-site of its own, devoted to one character, pairing, series, or other favorite subject. Depending on the corner of the web, the same thing might be called a fan page, fansite, tribute, dedication, or character page instead. Fan pages were widespread by the mid-1990s, and "shrine" circulated in some fandom communities too; it just wasn't a universal personal-web term.

Shrines became especially common in anime and manga fandom. Before anime became much easier to find in the West, the internet was one of the places fans went to find information about series that might never appear on local television or in an ordinary video store. A fan site could have character profiles, episode summaries, image galleries, translations, fan art, fanfiction, and links to fansubs or other sites devoted to the same series. Sometimes you had to already be online to discover any of this in the first place. One anime page led to another, then to a webring or directory, and suddenly there was an entire fandom for something you might never have encountered offline.

Graphics, Websets, and Linkware

You did not have to make every graphic or piece of a site yourself. Large libraries existed specifically to supply webmasters with parts. Randy's Icon Bazaar[25] distributed icons. Animation Factory[26] became a major source for the unmistakable rendered animated GIFs and 3D clipart that spread across personal sites. GRSites' Absolute Background Textures Archive[27] offered thousands of seamless backgrounds alongside buttons, bullets, arrows, rules, icons, and generated headings.

Graphics creators put together coordinated websets: a matching background, divider bars, bullets, headers, navigation buttons, and usually a small button crediting whoever made the set. Moyra's Web Jewels offered websets and other free graphics alongside hundreds of other sites distributing linkware, custom sets, and freebies.[28][29][30][31]

Linkware was a form of free web graphic distributed on the condition that the person using it linked back to its creator.[29][30] Creators set their own additional terms. They might require a credit button on the same page, forbid alterations, restrict commercial use, ask that a webset remain together, or prohibit redistribution. Permission to use a graphic did not include permission to repost it as part of your own collection. Tutorials and resource lists often directed visitors to the original site rather than re-uploading the files themselves.

The files were supposed to be saved and uploaded to your own server. "DO NOT DIRECT LINK" warnings were common because hotlinking somebody else's GIF made their server deliver it whenever somebody loaded your page, using their bandwidth instead of yours. It was routinely called bandwidth theft. Site owners could block outside requests or replace a hotlinked file with a warning graphic, making the offending site display whatever image the original owner chose to serve. Some creators included blank buttons or matching fonts so a set could be extended without altering the original graphics; others had extremely specific rules about what could be recolored, modified, mixed, or redistributed.

Personal sites often combined backgrounds, fonts, dolls, scripts, brushes, buttons, and other pieces made by different people. Credits and Resources pages listed those sources and linked back to their creators. Attribution was inconsistent, links died, and graphics escaped their original context constantly, but crediting the creator and linking back were established parts of the resource culture.

Layouts were distributed the same way. Creators offered complete layouts for other people to use and customize: change the colors, replace the main image, rewrite the content, keep the bones. The creator's credit and link back generally stayed with the layout, and the creator decided how extensively its code and graphics could be changed. That permission applied to layouts offered as resources, not to any layout somebody happened to see on another person's site. Copying someone's personal design or code without permission, removing somebody else's credit, or presenting their work as your own violated the same rules that applied to graphics. Some creators allowed extensive modification; others were considerably more "DO NOT TAKE MY CODE" about it.

The Social Web Before Social Media

Personal sites had their own social machinery long before feeds took over everything. Hit counters told you somebody had visited; guestbooks let them leave proof, usually with a name, website address, and sometimes a personalized calling card.[32][33]

By the early 2000s, shoutboxes and tagboards put a tiny public message stream directly on the page, somewhere between a guestbook and live chat. Services such as TagBoard were already turning up on personal sites by 2002, with Cbox following a little later. Site News and Updates pages gave the owner a running place to note whatever had just been added, changed, or redesigned.

Cliques and fanlistings[34] were easy to confuse because they worked in similar ways. A fanlisting was centered on one specific thing people were fans of, while a clique could be built around an interest, identity, joke, preference, or affiliation. The clearest difference was membership: most cliques expected you to have a website and display one of their buttons or codes, while a fanlisting did not require a website at all. The Fanlistings Network opened in May 2000 and became a central directory for them.[34] The buttons could feel quite different too. A Buffy the Vampire Slayer fanlisting badge meant, more or less, "I like Buffy." A clique badge reading "DESIGN WHORE" or "I ♥ MY COMPUTER" worked more like a tiny identity badge, which is probably why cliques feel closer to later blinkie and stamp culture.

Affiliates, sister or brother sites, and webrings connected personal sites to one another in slightly different ways. An affiliate usually meant some kind of ongoing link exchange, often between sites with similar subjects or audiences. Sister and brother sites could imply a closer relationship between the owners, but the terminology was loose: some people used "sister site," "affiliate," and even "banner exchange" for almost the same arrangement. A simple link exchange could be more impersonal still: you linked me, I linked you, and that was the whole relationship.

Webrings worked differently. Around since 1994,[84] they joined sites on a shared subject into a chain, with Previous, Next, Random, and similar links carrying visitors from one member to another. You could start on a Sailor Moon page, click Next enough times, and eventually work your way through an entire ring of Sailor Moon sites. The point was not just declaring membership; the ring was a way to actually move around the web.

Some sites kept an entire Joined page for all of this: cliques and fanlistings they belonged to, webrings they'd joined, affiliates, sister sites, campaigns, and whatever other little networks they had accumulated. These pages could run to dozens of buttons and memberships. Read through one and you got a pretty good picture of where that person spent their time online, what they liked, and which other sites they considered part of their neighborhood.

Website awards put one webmaster's approval on another person's page, at least in theory. Some had applications, published criteria, and an actual review before you were allowed to display the graphic. Others were handed out casually, and some were simply offered for anyone to take and put on their own site. Contest-winner badges, challenge awards, and participation graphics looked similar without necessarily meaning the same thing; sometimes you had won something, sometimes you had completed the challenge, and sometimes, with great distinction, you had shown up. Either way, the graphic became a little trophy on your page and often another link back to the person who made or issued it. Some people collected enough of them to need entire awards sections just to display them.

Between fanlistings, cliques, affiliates, sister sites, webrings, and awards, a personal site had many ways to describe a relationship: fan, member, ally, friend, endorsed. None of it had been flattened into one "follow" button.

None of that meant people didn't care about traffic. Topsites ranked member sites by visitor votes, so a "Vote for me" button asking people to click through and raise your rank was perfectly normal. Banner exchanges were more mechanical: you displayed other members' banners and, in return, yours appeared across their sites. These were about traffic rather than belonging, closer to the logic of a hit counter but organized and, in the case of topsites, competitive.

Graphics to Collect, Trade, and Display

A lot of these tiny graphics worked as identity markers. An 88×31 button could advertise a website, browser, cause, fandom, or affiliation. Later, stamps, fanlisting buttons, userbars, sig tags, and blinkies could tell you what somebody liked before you'd read a word they'd written. People filled whole pages with them because collecting them was part of the site.

Buttons, Badges, and Blinkies

One of the most characteristic uses of an 88×31 button was pointing back to your own site. A Link Me button meant: save this image to your own server and use it to link to my homepage. Webmasters often offered several designs, and sometimes several sizes, so their buttons would fit neatly alongside whatever a linking site already used. Unlike a fanlisting or clique badge, a Link Me button represented the site itself. The same 88×31 format was also used for "Best viewed in Netscape" and "Best viewed in Internet Explorer" badges, software and hosting logos, and W3C validation buttons.

The smaller 80×15 antipixel or brilliant button appeared in late 2002 and spread quickly, especially through blogs. Its tiny size made it easy to stack whole rows of them together. They advertised browsers, operating systems, editors, web standards, RSS and Atom feeds, software, directories, hosts, and other sites, but they were just as useful for declaring interests, affiliations, preferences, or jokes. A row of them could end up reading like a miniature profile.

Blinkies went in more than one direction. One familiar form was the narrow animated banner, usually around 150×20, with a border or other part of the graphic flashing between frames. Another was built from individual letter blocks, sometimes with tiny icons added, with the letters or blocks lighting up, changing color, bouncing, or moving in sequence. Surviving examples show the block style in use by 2003, particularly around doll sites and later Neopets fansites, and tutorials describe blinkies as being assembled from separate "blocks, icons and letters."[35][36][37] The two forms continued alongside each other rather than one simply replacing the other. The block version is now often called a chain, block, or bead blinkie; Glitter-Graphics still files them under Chain Blinkies.[38][39] On Neocities and other old-web revival sites, "blinkie" more often means the 150×20 banner again, while 88×31 has settled into its own category as a website button.[40][20] The name stayed the same even as the graphic changed.

Adoptables and Trading

Adoptables were made to be collected. Artists offered animals, dolls, fantasy creatures, food, plants, toys, and almost anything else for visitors to "adopt," save to their own server, and display on their website, usually with a link back to the artist. Collections often ended up on pages called toyboxes or adoption centers. The pets themselves, also called cyberpets or virtual pets, might come with names, personalities, certificates, care instructions, or a tiny fictional habitat.

Some mystery eggs and hatchables added an actual surprise. Instead of choosing the finished creature, you adopted an egg and waited to see what it became. Some worked by having the adopter display an image from the artist's server so the artist could later replace the egg file with its hatched form; the URL stayed the same, but the graphic on your page changed. There was no guarantee what you would get. It was a tiny web-graphics gachapon.

People also made graphics specifically for each other. Gifts, freebies, trades, personalized site titles, dolls, buttons, and signatures were part of the social life around personal sites. Someone might keep an entire page of graphics made for them by friends or visitors, with each one linked back to whoever made it.

The Quilting Bee (Q*Bee)[48], founded in 2000 and active through 2014,[49] turned pixel trading into a club of its own. Members made patches, traded them with one another, and "sewed" the patches they received onto pixel quilts displayed on their websites. The club also ran monthly pixel challenges, blog-a-thons, story writing, holiday-card exchanges, and other activities, with patches and pixel mushrooms given out for participating.

Online TCGs did something similar with collectible image cards. The community generally credits Senshi Card-Mania!, opened January 22, 2002, as the first fan-run online TCG.[88] Members earned randomized cards through games and site activities, traded duplicates with one another, and kept a trade post on their own website showing what they owned, what they were collecting, and what they had available. Your website was your binder. The hobby eventually produced layouts made specifically for trade posts and scripts for keeping increasingly large collections organized.

A few years later, clickable adoptables turned the same idea into more of a game. You adopted an egg or pet and put its image and link in a forum signature, profile, or website; other people's views or clicks could help it hatch, grow, get fed, or level up. Dragon Cave[41], launched in 2006, became one of the best-known examples, followed by sites and systems such as Valenth, Squiby, MagiStream, and later PokéFarm. Forum signatures could fill up with creatures waiting for clicks, which helped the pet grow while advertising the adoption site everywhere the owner posted.[42]

Dollz and Pixel Communities

KiSS (Kisekae Set System) predates The Palace. The KiSS/GS specification says the paper-doll program had been in development for NEC PC-9801VM2 computers since March 1991.[85] A KiSS set packaged a doll and its clothing into its own file format, which could then be opened in a dedicated viewer and dressed by dragging the pieces into place. It began in Japan and spread to other computer platforms during the 1990s, with later versions adding animation, sound, and other interactive features. It belongs to the same larger history of dressing little digital people, but technically it was its own system and had nothing to do with Palace props.

The web culture that became known as Dollz grew out of The Palace, the graphical chat system opened to the public in 1995.[87] Palace avatars were assembled from 44×44 graphics called props; a user could wear up to nine at once within a 132×132 area. Early Dollz often used three vertically stacked props for the body, leaving the others for hair, clothing, and accessories. These could be layered onto the body in the Palace prop editor or drawn directly onto the base. Individual Palaces kept collections of saved avatars, often sorted into types like Preps, Sk8rs, Silents, and whatever else that particular community had accumulated. Early names included "lil people" and paper dolls before "Dollz" became the name most widely associated with them on the web.

Once Dollz moved out of The Palace and onto personal websites, they no longer had to work as Palace avatars. GeoCities, Angelfire, and independent domains filled up with finished dolls, downloadable bases, clothing, tutorials, contests, adoptions, requests, and drag-and-drop dollmakers. A dollmaker usually gave you a base surrounded by different hair, clothes, shoes, and accessories to layer onto it, after which the finished doll could be saved and used on your own site. People edited them, traded them, adopted them, entered them into contests, and made new pieces for one another. Crediting the artist who made the base or other parts was an important part of the culture. Dollz Mania and The Doll Palace became two of the larger sites in a much wider network of personal Dollz sites.[43][44]

By the early 2000s, dolling had also become a digital-art hobby of its own. Some artists drew whole bases, clothing, hair, and poses themselves, either from scratch or on top of another artist's credited base. In parts of the community, dolls or pixel dolls became preferable to "Dollz" for this kind of work, partly to distinguish hand-drawn dolls from things assembled in a dollmaker. The terminology was never very tidy. "Dollz," "dolls," "pixel dolls," and "cartoon dollz" all turn up, and "dolls" alone is a terrible search term if what you actually want is a tiny illustrated person from somebody's 2003 homepage. More specific names often tell you more anyway: Preps, Sk8rs, Silents, Minis, Wonderkins, Divas, and plenty of others referred to recognizable body styles, poses, or base families.

What English-language doll sites later called Korean Dollz came from a different kind of system. Korean community and gaming sites were building commercial avatar systems around the turn of the 2000s. SayClub began offering avatars in 2000 and started selling clothing and accessories for them that November; Freechal, Cyworld, and other services developed their own avatar economies around the same period. These were not open bases for hobbyists to draw on. The bodies, hairstyles, clothing, and accessories were professionally made for the service, and users built an avatar from the pieces available to them, often buying individual items as virtual goods. Cyworld's MiniMe lived inside the user's minihompy and decorated miniroom, so the avatar became part of both the user's appearance and the little space they built around it.

Candybar[45] was one of the Korean avatar systems that became especially visible to Western doll sites. Developed by Neople, it opened in beta on 21 December 2001 as an avatar and gaming portal, with games including Love Love Studio and Kung Kung Tta. Around 2004, its artwork began circulating much more widely in English-language dolling through eLouai's unofficial Candybar Doll Maker.[46] eLouai took the existing Candybar assets and turned them into a browser-based dress-up maker, letting users combine the bodies, faces, hair, clothes, shoes, accessories, pets, backgrounds, and other pieces that Candybar's artists had already drawn.

That was quite different from the way Western Dollz were usually made. Dollers regularly drew new clothing, hair, bases, and accessories for one another, but Candybar users mostly worked with the existing Candybar library. The artwork was much denser and more detailed than most of the small hand-drawn Dollz circulating on personal sites, with carefully shaded clothes, tiny accessories, glossy highlights, and comparatively large heads. Reproducing that style well was difficult, and making new Candybar-compatible pieces was never the main activity. The attraction was the enormous collection of professionally drawn parts that were already there to combine. People sometimes edited a finished avatar afterward, but they generally were not building a new Candybar wardrobe themselves.

Western sites still absorbed the finished avatars into Dollz culture, collecting them beside Preps, Silents, pixel dolls, and other styles. "Korean Dollz" seems to have been mainly an English-language collector term; the Korean services themselves generally called them avatars and the individual clothes and accessories avatar items. Candybar was only one source. Similar graphics came from services including Cyworld, SayClub, Netmarble, Hangame, Daum, Buddy Buddy, and Freechal. Much of that material is harder to reconstruct now because the original avatar systems were tied to user accounts and pages the Wayback Machine could not fully reach.

Beyond the Homepage: Forums, Messaging, and Profiles

The same graphics culture moved well beyond homepages. Forums, AIM, LiveJournal, and later MySpace ran alongside personal domains for years. Website buttons became profile badges and stamps. Guestbooks and comments coexisted. Doll and pixel-art communities appeared in avatars and icons as easily as on a homepage. Graphics sites added Photoshop brushes, textures, and templates beside the older GIFs and websets, serving whichever platform you happened to be decorating that week.

Personal homepages didn't get replaced overnight by something unrelated. For a while, the graphics culture just moved with its users onto whatever surface they logged into next. Writing in 2007, Lialina described people moving out of standalone homepages into "the language of MySpace" — accounts, profiles, journals, channels — and pointed out how much those new pages still resembled their Web 1.0 predecessors: a background, blocks of graphics, text, and video laid over it, held together by the same modular, HTML-based aesthetic. She specifically named glitter graphics as the look that had come to define this new amateur web, in contrast to the starry backgrounds of the 1990s homepage.[91] MySpace centralized where people lived online well before it centralized what their pages looked like.

Forums offered two main spots to customize: an avatar beside your username and a signature under your posts. They could match as a set or have nothing to do with each other. Graphics boards often bundled requests for both, handled by the same pool of designers.

The format depended on the platform, and people kept accounts—and icons—on several at once. Forum avatars followed whatever limits the board imposed. AIM buddy icons ran 48×48 or 50×50, MSN Messenger display pictures were 96×96, and LiveJournal userpics were 100×100. Dedicated sites supplied all of it, collecting AIM icons, MSN pictures, Yahoo smileys, and away messages by the thousands.

Signatures were the customizable block automatically attached beneath forum posts. They could be one line of text or a quote, but people often treated the space like a miniature profile: custom graphics, usernames, favorite characters, animations, links, whatever the board's limits allowed. Making signatures became its own branch of web graphics. You could make one yourself, ask a friend, or post in a request thread where a designer took your username, character or image, colors, text, and general direction, then posted the finished version back, sometimes with revisions or several options.

The graphic inside a forum signature was often called a sig tag. It usually centered on a character or other artwork, sometimes with a name or username worked into the design. People made them in programs such as Paint Shop Pro and Photoshop using swirl, grunge, sparkle, and vector brushes, along with pixel art, glitter, decorative type, and Paint Shop Pro tubes. You generally used one until you made or requested a new one to replace it.

Salvaged, maintained by Belle, archives brushes from older graphics programs, including the kinds of assets used to make graphics like these.

By the mid-2000s, userbars had become another forum-signature staple: long, thin 350×19 banners, usually with a tiny image on one side and text on the other, announcing whatever the bar was about. Because they were only 19 pixels tall, you could stack several of them inside a forum's signature-height limit. They were also much easier to collect and swap around than a full custom signature graphic.

Stamps have a much cleaner origin. DeviantArt's then-creative director Ryan Ford created the format for the 2005 DeviantArt Summit, originally planned for an airplane hangar; the little air-mail or postage-style badge made sense for the event. The two earliest recovered examples were posted on April 22, 2005.[86] The standard became a 99×56 graphic framed by that recognizable perforated postage-stamp border, either static or animated.

Users almost immediately turned stamps into tiny visual bumper stickers for their identities and opinions. They covered fandoms, ships, software, music, games, political and social views, characters, aesthetics, jokes, and every variety of "I support…" or "Proud ___" declaration. Some had edgy Happy Bunny or Spencer's-tee energy; others were practical DeviantArt etiquette, telling visitors to view the full artwork, not steal it, leave feedback, stop repeatedly viewing a page without watching the account, or check whether requests and commissions were open.

The stamp was usually uploaded as a DeviantArt deviation in its own right. Other users didn't have to save and re-upload the GIF to display it. They could embed the original deviation with thumbnail syntax such as :thumb427548925:, so the stamp on their profile still pointed back to the creator's submission.

Iconator[50] collected huge libraries of AIM buddy icons, avatars, wallpapers, and away messages. Glitter-Graphics.com[51] and Dazzle Junction[52][53] supplied glitter text, backgrounds, layouts, and copy-and-paste code for MySpace and other profile sites. FlamingText[54] generated customized headings and animated text from whatever you typed into the box. The surface changed; the habit didn't. Forums, LiveJournal, Xanga, Piczo, Gaia, Neopets, MySpace, and AIM each gave people a slightly different place to put an avatar, a signature, a background, a profile layout, a comment, a badge, personalized text, or an identity graphic — the same handful of behaviors turning up again on a new surface every time users moved somewhere new.

You could take a background from one graphics site, a premade iframe layout from another teenager, learn how to change its scrollbar from Funky Chickens, grab an ALT character from CYP2, add an official Neopets GIF, put a Candybar doll beside the guestbook, and finish with a row of buttons linking back to everybody whose work you'd used. Of course it looked eclectic. Half the page came from different people.

The graphics did a lot of the social work themselves. A button sent you to somebody else's site; an award showed that another webmaster had approved of yours; a sig tag or blinkie said something about you without a paragraph of explanation. Even a tiled background could carry one person's work into hundreds of other people's websites.

Some formats really were defined by their dimensions, so the useful ones are worth keeping straight:

Format Common / canonical size Why this size?
Website button ("Link Me") 88×31 Popularized by early web badges, including Netscape's promotional buttons, and later recognized by the IAB as the "Micro Bar." The exact dimensions were a convention rather than a browser limitation.
Antipixel button 80×15 Established by Jeremy Hedley's Antipixel buttons in the early 2000s. The deliberately tiny format worked well for weblog sidebars and spread as a reusable template rather than because of a technical restriction.
Fanlisting code 50×50, 75×50, 100×35, 100×50 (sometimes 88×31 or 200×40) There was no single required size. Fanlistings offered several standardized code sizes so members could choose one that fit their own site's layout, and those dimensions became conventions across the community.
DeviantArt stamp 99×56 Originated with DeviantArt's 2005 Summit promotion as a tiny digital stamp or bumper sticker. The original 99×56 template was copied for user-made stamps until the dimensions became part of the format; it was not a DeviantArt image-size limit.
Userbar 350×19 Designed for forum signatures: wide enough for a label and small graphic, but short enough that several could be stacked without taking over a post. The exact 350×19 dimensions became a community template standard rather than a universal forum restriction.
Neocities-style banner blinkie 150×20 The classic blinkie template was long enough for a short line of animated text while remaining tiny enough to stack with other graphics. The dimensions were a community convention, not a Neocities restriction.
Web banner (banner exchange) 468×60 Standardized as the web advertising "full banner," allowing the same ad to circulate through banner exchanges and unrelated websites without being resized. It was later formalized as an IAB standard ad size.
AIM buddy icon 48×48 or 50×50 A client-imposed small icon size, roughly in line with contemporary desktop GUI icons and compact enough to sit inside a chat interface while keeping downloads small. AOL does not appear to have documented why it chose exactly 48–50 pixels.
MSN display picture 96×96 Messenger displayed and resized profile pictures to 96×96 as part of its interface design. The dimension also matches Windows' traditional 96-DPI baseline, although Microsoft does not appear to have documented that as the reason it chose 96 pixels.
LiveJournal userpic 100×100 A platform maximum suited to LiveJournal's text-heavy layout: large enough to work as an expressive image, but small enough to appear repeatedly beside posts and comments. The site's interface became heavily structured around the 100×100 limit, although the original reason for choosing exactly 100 pixels does not appear to be documented.
The Palace prop / maximum avatar area 44×44 / 132×132 The Palace was built around a fixed 44×44 sprite tile: the default roundhead and each wearable prop occupied one tile. An avatar could combine up to nine props across a 3×3 area, producing the 132×132 maximum. Small fixed graphics also suited the memory, bandwidth, and processing constraints of the time, although the reason 44 pixels specifically was chosen does not appear to be documented.

Blingee arrived after years of people making, collecting, modifying, trading, and reusing small pieces of visual material online. It did not invent that behavior; it pulled the process into one tool. Earlier, building a personal page out of reusable pieces meant assembling a whole website: a tiled background from one creator, buttons from another, a doll from somewhere else, a blinkie, some copied HTML, and your own text and photos. Blingee moved that same modular habit inside a single image instead of a whole page. Rather than assembling a website from reusable pieces, you assembled one picture from reusable pieces — glitter animations, borders, text, stars, hearts, characters, decorative overlays, a photograph, and stamps other members had uploaded. Users uploaded stamps to the site, where other members could use them in collages of their own.

Blingee

Blingee[55] was a web platform for making glitter collages; individual creations were called Blingees. Launched in 2006 as part of the Bauer Teen Network[56], its browser-based editor let users layer decorative "stamps"—from animated glitter and twinkling stars to complex image overlays—onto uploaded photographs. Bauer Publishing also owned the teen magazine J-14, so it clearly understood the demographic.

Decorating personal photos with glittery stamps had a physical parallel. In Japan, purikura[57] booths had been popular since the mid-1990s, letting users embellish their photos with cute decorations, sparkles, and frames. Purikura lived in arcades and Blingee lived online, but both offered maximalist self-expression through layered ornamentation.

Blingee built a community around groups, contests, and ratings, but its tools point outward as much as inward. It offered image dimensions optimized for MySpace profile pictures alongside built-in sharing to MySpace, Hi5[58], and Piczo[59]. Blingee wasn't a standalone art site whose images happened to spread elsewhere afterward — its export sizes and sharing buttons were built around the specific profile-based platforms its users were already decorating and communicating through.

A Blingee stamp carried the same older habit forward in platform-native form: one person uploads a visual object, and other people incorporate that object into their own personal creation. Earlier web creators set their own terms for that kind of reuse — credit required, no alterations, no redistribution outside the original webset — and Blingee replaced that patchwork with a single platform rule instead. Uploading a stamp made it available to the whole site, permanently: once another member had used a stamp, the uploader could no longer remove it. Users built personal collections and uploaded custom stamps for other members to use, carrying the older culture of shared web graphics into an increasingly centralized social web, just with reuse built into the software rather than negotiated one webset at a time.

The platform nearly shut down in August 2015. User outcry followed, new funding kept it online, and previously premium features became free. Blingee finally closed in June 2024. The surviving closure notice did not give a reason, so attributing the shutdown to one specific cause would be guesswork.

The Web 2.0 Transition

The move from personal websites to social media wasn't a single jump from homepages to standardized platforms. It passed through a middle stage of customizable, centralized profiles, and MySpace is the important transitional case: it centralized identity and social interaction inside one company's service while still handing users unusual control over how their own page looked, an extension of the homepage habit rather than a break from it — the continuity Lialina was already tracking in 2007.[91] Facebook[60] represented a different model of online identity. MySpace allowed extensive profile personalization; Facebook offered almost none. The broader shift called Web 2.0[61] emphasized user-generated content inside centralized platforms rather than user-built pages, and increasingly meant the platform controlling how that content looked rather than the user. Meanwhile, Mozilla Firefox[62] challenged Internet Explorer[63] in a browser market that had gone stagnant. Firefox joined a broader standards movement that pushed development away from "best viewed in IE" and toward cross-browser design based on specifications from the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C).[64] Glitter graphics flourished specifically in the window when centralized platforms still gave users somewhere to put decorative external images, custom backgrounds, HTML, layouts, comments, and profile pictures. As that window narrowed and users migrated further into standardized social networks, the personal homepage began to look antiquated.

Lycos kept the remaining Tripod and Angelfire sites online long after the free-hosting era ended. By early January 2026, Angelfire's user pages had entered a prolonged outage and its homepage worked only sporadically. On March 6, a short-lived notice on the Lycos homepage said Angelfire and Tripod would shut down within 30 days and told users to move their hosting elsewhere. Lycos removed the notice soon afterward, although it remained in the page source, then restored it on April 3.[65] Both services went offline on April 24, 2026, ending nearly three decades of web hosting.

As major free hosts closed or moved to paid plans, glitter graphics migrated instead of disappearing. They persisted across MySpace, Tumblr[66], Facebook, and WhatsApp, where image-based greetings became a daily ritual for many users. Circulated globally and often multilingual, these graphics mark mornings, holidays, birthdays, religious observances, and other everyday milestones: a lightweight but emotionally resonant way to maintain social bonds across distance. They pair coffee cups, flowers, and decorative typography with animated glitter and sparkles, earnest affection dressed in visual excess.

The animation tends to get lost along the way. Screenshooting a graphic instead of saving the original file immediately turns it into a static image. A screenshot may catch phone or app UI at the edges, and each crop eats farther into the graphic. Repeated downloads and re-uploads add compression artifacts until something that began crisp and animated ends up blurry, pixelated, and still. "Deep frying" describes the deliberate, exaggerated version of that damage—not every ordinary loss of quality.

Impact

Today they resurface in nostalgic internet communities and on hosts such as Neocities[67], where people deliberately revive early web aesthetics. Glitter graphics also survive in searchable GIF libraries such as Tenor[68] and GIPHY[69]. When Google closed Tenor's public API in June 2026, some third-party apps moved to GIPHY or newer services such as Klipy.

Some vocabulary drifted along the way. Modern Neocities guides may define a "shrine" as any enthusiastically curated page about something the webmaster loves—Pokémon, a book, a tabletop campaign, anything. That meaning is broader than the character- and fandom-specific shrines found on older personal sites, and the term has become a standard part of the revival scene's vocabulary. Useful term, but it is a revival-era codification of an old fandom habit, not something every GeoCities user had sitting next to their toybox page back in the day.

Saving the files isn't the same as saving what they meant. Lialina made this point about the wider early web: collecting old MIDI files and animated GIFs preserves the artifacts, but not necessarily what problems they solved, how people actually used them, or which communities they belonged to.[90] That's part of why this page spends so much time on linkware, dolling, websets, fanlistings, adoptables, signatures, and MySpace layouts instead of just the glitter graphics themselves — the images tend to survive better than the practices that surrounded them.

The look has made its way into contemporary art and pop culture, especially Net Art[70] and work that deliberately revives early web aesthetics. The video for M.I.A.[71]'s 2010 single XXXO[72] references Blingee directly through glittering floral graphics and decorative stamps.

A lot has been lost. Resources vanish as archives age and the personal hosts that carried them disappear; even when a page survives, its externally hosted images often do not. The loss or restriction of image hosts such as Boomspeed[73], TinyPic[74], and Photobucket[75] stings because limited storage and bandwidth led many site owners to keep images off-site. TinyPic shut down; Photobucket survives as a paid service, but its policy changes replaced countless old embeds with giant watermarks or upgrade notices. The HTML remained while the page's face disappeared.

Technical Foundation

The GIF[76] format, introduced in 1987 by CompuServe[77], could store multiple images from the beginning. The 1989 revision added timing and disposal controls that made browser animation practical. Combined with broad browser support, those features made GIF the foundation of animated glitter graphics. Steve Wilhite[78] insisted the name is pronounced like Jif, sparking one of the internet's most heated debates. I pronounce GIF with a hard "G" and locked in that pronunciation by age six, so he should've spoken up sooner. May he rest in peace though.

Early glitter graphics were made with programs such as Adobe ImageReady[79] and Jasc Paint Shop Pro[80] with its companion GIF program, Animation Shop.[81] Later, Blingee, PicMix[82], and Online Image Editor[83] let people make similar graphics in a browser. PicMix and Online Image Editor remain available; Blingee closed in 2024. The surviving tools felt too clunky and outdated for me to enjoy, which is why I built Glitter Graphics. No shade.
