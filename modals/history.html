History

Glitter Graphics are animated graphics characterized by looping sparkle and glitter effects applied to text, illustrations, or photographs.

As a form of maximalist[1] digital vernacular art, glitter graphics sit comfortably inside both camp[2] and kitsch[3]: excessive, sincere, decorative, and eventually very far from the minimalism that took over mainstream web design. They were everywhere through the 2000s, especially on amateur websites and early social-media profiles where people could still control what their pages looked like.

Origins and Early Development

There doesn't seem to be one clean first Glitter Graphic. By the early 2000s they were circulating through online forum signatures and the personal-site platforms that had already put ordinary people online in huge numbers: GeoCities[4], AOL Hometown[5], Tripod[6], Angelfire[7], and Homestead[8]. People made them, collected them from other creators, reposted them, and wrote tutorials for making your own.

The style spread across a ridiculous range of subjects, from Disney illustration to emo and scene[9] to McBling[10]. A lot of the early examples share the same basic construction: a short sparkle animation, often reused with different colors, laid over text or an existing illustration.

As online communities multiplied, many still let users customize their pages with HTML and CSS, a level of control that's mostly gone from modern social platforms like Instagram and Facebook. Glitter graphics fit perfectly into those spaces: Neopets[11] petpages, userlookups, and guilds, then Gaia Online[12] and MySpace[13] profiles after both launched in 2003.

The Personal Web Aesthetic

The look ranged all over the place, and I don't think chaos was really the default. Plenty of people had real taste and a genuine eye for design, and a personal site was often where that eye actually got trained, one bad color choice and one too many blinking GIFs at a time. But maximalism was always an option nobody stopped you from taking: tiled backgrounds fighting five different fonts, animated glitter piled on more animated glitter, a MIDI file autoplaying in the background whether you asked for it or not. Nothing was designed by committee, so a site could be genuinely well put together, a complete mess, or both at once depending which page you scrolled to.

Traffic and visibility still mattered a lot, too. Guestbook signatures, topsite votes, banner clicks, a hit counter creeping upward: that stuff mattered to basically every webmaster, and there was real money in it too, whole businesses selling premade layouts and design work. What there wasn't was one algorithm deciding who got seen. Nobody's homepage got buried or boosted by an engagement score, so nobody was really building for distribution. They were building for whoever showed up. That's a lot of why no two sites ever really looked alike: no platform template flattening every page into the same shape, just one person at a time deciding, in Notepad or a WYSIWYG editor or whatever inline code box their host gave them, what their little corner of the internet should look like. Some people even wrote a whole "why I made this site" manifesto explaining themselves, sometimes running to genuine essay length, over something they'd built purely because they wanted to.

Building a Personal Website

GeoCities was the best-known free host, but Tripod, Angelfire, Homestead, AOL Hometown, and countless smaller services did the same basic job. "GeoCities" eventually became shorthand for a whole style of amateur web design: personal sites assembled from whatever graphics, code, and free resources their owner could scrounge up. GeoCities itself leaned hard into the idea of a website as a place of your own. Its users were homesteaders, and early sites lived at numbered addresses inside themed neighborhoods such as Hollywood, Area51, Tokyo, and WestHollywood.[14][15]

One common professional workflow was to design for an 800 × 600 screen in Photoshop, slice the page apart in ImageReady or Fireworks, then rebuild it in HTML tables.[16][17] Teen and hobbyist designers picked up the same tricks secondhand. By the early 2000s, premade table, iframe, and DIV layouts circulated freely, often built around one large image with a tiny scrolling content area dropped into it. Navigation might be an image map or rollover links laid over the artwork. Splash pages asking you to "Enter," microscopic text, custom scrollbars, and layouts built around one photo or illustration were everywhere, and people kept whole collections of layouts to download, edit, and reuse.[18]

In the design-heavy personal-domain scene, especially among teen and young-adult designers, the look shifted again around 2002. Iframes and image maps got more common, text got even tinier, and a three-way nav split started appearing everywhere: me / you / www, roughly meaning about-me, for-visitors, and links-out. Navigation felt much more deliberately designed than the wander-and-discover pages of a few years earlier, which was refreshing at the time even if some of it has aged terribly. Sites also changed constantly; people would redo a whole layout every few weeks because they'd found something better. If a friend had actual paid hosting, they'd often give you a subdomain, which meant owning a good .com that people would actually want attached to their name suddenly mattered.

There was a huge number of tiny graphics made just for building websites: tiled backgrounds, animated dividers, bullets, arrows, clipart, pixel sprites, matching Home / About / Links / Guestbook buttons, hit counters, email graphics, welcome signs, Online / Away / Updated graphics, and flashing "Under Construction" banners. Little "NEW!" icons pointed at whatever had changed. Background MIDI, then WAVs and MP3s, made the page audible too. "Best viewed in Netscape," "Best viewed at 800 × 600," browser badges, HTML-validation buttons, "Made with Notepad" graphics, and dated update logs were all normal.[19][20][21] Then came the gimmicks: marquees, cursor trails, clocks, status-bar messages, disabled right-clicks, rollover images, pop-ups, splash screens, browser-specific entrances. Personal sites rarely felt finished because people kept adding things to them, which probably explains why "Under Construction" survived for so long.

Code moved around almost as freely as the graphics. Funky Chickens HTML Help[22], created in 1999, broke HTML into little effects you could copy straight into your page. Lissa Explains it All was an even earlier pioneer: a colorful, easy-to-follow HTML tutorial site launched in 1997 by 11-year-old Melissa "Lissa" Brown specifically to help kids and beginners build webpages without an automatic page builder. Her notes began on AOL, moved to GeoCities as the site grew, and then moved to their own domain when she was 12. CYP2 / ColorYourProfyle[23] did something similar for AOL profiles and webpages: color tables, moving and fading text, ALT characters, Wingdings and Webdings, font codes, premade profiles, webpage dolls, and whatever else could be squeezed into AOL's tiny editable boxes. You didn't necessarily sit down to "learn web development." You learned enough HTML to change the background, copied a marquee from somewhere, found a color code somewhere else, broke the page, and eventually figured out why. A lot of that learning was just right-clicking someone's page and hitting "View Source" to see how they'd done something, then lifting the part you needed. That's also the actual reason all those disabled-right-click scripts existed: not a mystery gimmick, just an attempt to stop exactly that. It didn't always work, either as a deterrent or as a lock. At seven years old, I copied code straight off a site that hadn't bothered with one, and still got called out for it in my own guestbook.

Some platforms encouraged this directly. Neopets let users customize petpages, userlookups, shops, and guild pages, and kept its own Fun Images[24] library: animated Neopets graphics with the HTML already written underneath, ready to copy and paste. Giving people both the graphic and the code to decorate their little part of a site was completely normal.

Some pages existed for one specific obsession. A character shrine might devote a whole page or mini-site to one character, pairing, or piece of media. Depending on the corner of the web, the same thing might be called a fan page, fansite, tribute, dedication, or character page instead. Fan pages were already everywhere by the mid-1990s, and "shrine" was circulating in some fandom communities too; it just wasn't a universal personal-web term.

Graphics, Websets, and Linkware

You did not have to make every graphic or piece of a site yourself. Huge libraries existed specifically to supply webmasters with parts. Randy's Icon Bazaar[25] distributed icons. Animation Factory[26] became a major source for the unmistakable rendered animated GIFs and 3D clipart that spread across personal sites. GRSites' Absolute Background Textures Archive[27] offered thousands of seamless backgrounds alongside buttons, bullets, arrows, rules, icons, and generated headings.

Graphics creators put together coordinated websets: a matching background, divider bars, bullets, headers, navigation buttons, and usually a small button crediting whoever made the set. Moyra's Web Jewels offered websets and other free graphics alongside hundreds of other sites distributing linkware, custom sets, and freebies.[28][29][30][31]

Linkware was a form of free web graphic distributed on the condition that the person using it linked back to its creator.[29][30] Creators set their own additional terms. They might require a credit button on the same page, forbid alterations, restrict commercial use, ask that a webset remain together, or prohibit redistribution. Permission to use a graphic did not include permission to repost it as part of your own collection. Tutorials and resource lists often directed visitors to the original site rather than re-uploading the files themselves.

The files were supposed to be saved and uploaded to your own server. "DO NOT DIRECT LINK" appeared everywhere because hotlinking somebody else's GIF made their server deliver the file every time somebody loaded your page, using their bandwidth instead of yours. It was routinely called bandwidth theft. Site owners could block outside requests or replace hotlinked images with a warning graphic, which meant the offending site displayed whatever image the original owner chose to serve instead. Some creators included blank buttons or matching fonts so a set could be extended without altering the original graphics; others had extremely specific rules about what could be recolored, modified, mixed, or redistributed.

Personal sites often combined backgrounds, fonts, dolls, scripts, brushes, buttons, and other pieces made by different people. Credits and Resources pages listed those sources and linked back to their creators. Attribution was inconsistent, links died, and graphics escaped their original context constantly, but crediting the creator and linking back were established parts of the resource culture.

Layouts were distributed the same way. Creators offered complete layouts for other people to use and customize: change the colors, replace the main image, rewrite the content, keep the bones. The creator's credit and link back generally stayed with the layout, and the creator decided how extensively its code and graphics could be changed. That permission applied to layouts offered as resources, not to any layout somebody happened to see on another person's site. Copying someone's personal design or code without permission, removing somebody else's credit, or presenting their work as your own violated the same rules that applied to graphics. Some creators allowed extensive modification; others were considerably more "DO NOT TAKE MY CODE" about it.

The Social Web Before Social Media

Personal sites had their own social machinery long before feeds took over everything. Hit counters told you somebody had visited; guestbooks let them leave proof, usually with a name, website address, and sometimes a personalized calling card.[32][33]

By the early 2000s, shoutboxes and tagboards put a tiny public message stream directly on the page, somewhere between a guestbook and live chat; Cbox became one of the best-known embeds. Site News and Updates pages did the owner's side of the same job: a running log of whatever had just been added or changed.

Cliques and fanlistings[34] got confused constantly, and honestly, for good reason. A fanlisting is basically a clique organized around being a fan of one specific thing. The practical difference was membership: a clique usually expected you to have a website and belong to the group; a fanlisting only needed your name and country, because it was counting fans rather than sites. The Fanlistings Network opened in May 2000 and became a central directory for them.[34] The buttons said different things too. A Buffy the Vampire Slayer fanlisting badge basically meant "I like Buffy." A clique badge reading "DESIGN WHORE" or "I ♥ MY COMPUTER" worked more like a tiny identity badge, which is probably why cliques feel closer to later blinkie and stamp culture.

Affiliates, sister or brother sites, and webrings connected sites rather than people to a club. An affiliate was a site you had an ongoing link relationship with, usually because the sites were related or the owners supported each other's work. A sister or brother site implied something closer: a webmaster you actually had a relationship with, sometimes sharing visitors, projects, or hosting, though nobody agreed on exactly how close you had to be. A link exchange could be much more impersonal: "I'll link you if you link me." Webrings, around since 1994, worked differently again.[84] Previous / next / random links wired related sites into a browsable chain, so the whole point was clicking through to the next one.

Some sites kept an entire Joined page listing the cliques and fanlistings they belonged to, the webrings they'd joined, and the affiliates and sister sites they had, and it wasn't unusual for a page like that to run to dozens of memberships and links at once. Read through one and you got a pretty good picture of where that person wandered around online.

Website awards put one webmaster's approval on another person's page. A Gold Award, Cool Site award, or Site of the Month graphic usually meant somebody had looked at your site and decided it was good enough to vouch for, sometimes after an application and actual review, sometimes basically for the asking. Contest-winner badges, challenge awards, and participation graphics looked similar without necessarily meaning the same thing; sometimes you had simply completed the challenge or, with great distinction, shown up. Either way, the graphic became a trophy on your page and another link back to the person who issued it.

Between fanlistings, cliques, affiliates, sister sites, webrings, and awards, a personal site had a lot of different ways to say what it was to something or someone (fan, member, ally, friend, endorsed) instead of flattening all of it into one "follow" button.

None of that meant people didn't care about traffic. Topsites ranked member sites by visitor votes, so a "Vote for me" button asking people to click through and raise your rank was perfectly normal. Banner exchanges were more mechanical: you displayed other members' banners and, in return, yours appeared across their sites. These were about traffic rather than belonging, closer to the logic of a hit counter but organized and, in the case of topsites, competitive.

Graphics to Collect, Trade, and Display

A lot of these tiny graphics worked as identity markers. An 88×31 button could advertise a website, browser, cause, fandom, or affiliation. Later, stamps, fanlisting buttons, userbars, sig tags, and blinkies could tell you what somebody liked before you'd read a word they'd written. People filled whole pages with them because collecting them was part of the site.

Buttons, Badges, and Blinkies

One of the most characteristic uses of an 88×31 was pointing back at your own site. A Link Me button meant: save this to your own server and link it to my homepage. Webmasters often offered several designs, sometimes in several sizes because different button walls had different rules; one surviving dollmaking site offers 130×55, 130×50, and 88×31 versions for exactly that reason. Unlike a fanlisting or clique badge, a Link Me button represented the site itself. The same 88×31 shape also got reused for "Best viewed in Netscape" and "Best viewed in Internet Explorer" badges, software and hosting logos, and W3C standards buttons.

A smaller cousin, the 80×15 antipixel or brilliant button, caught on around late 2002. It was tiny enough to stack in rows and dense enough to function like a miniature bio: browser and screen resolution, web standards, RSS or Atom feeds, directories, webrings, blogrolls, software, languages, editors, operating systems, hosts, locations, beliefs, games, sports teams, music, food. Plenty were just jokes.

Blinkies are messy, and I think it's because the name got applied to two different graphics at different points. Going by the name itself, the flashing-border banner probably has the better claim to being first: a narrow 150×20 animated strip that actually blinks. The letter-block version seems to have come along later, spelling a name or short phrase out of individual blocks that lit up, changed color, or bounced in sequence, almost like beads on a bracelet, and it got a life of its own on doll sites and Neopets fansites. Tutorials describe them as separate "blocks, icons and letters," and examples survive from 2003.[35][36] These are now usually called chain, block, or bead blinkies; Glitter-Graphics still has a whole Chain Blinkies category.[37][38][39] On Neocities and other old-web revival sites, "blinkie" circled back to something closer to the original flashing-banner sense, with 88×31 covering the button shape instead.[40][20] Both graphics existed under the same name at different points. The terminology just kept moving.

Adoptables and Trading

Adoptables made the collecting literal. Artists offered animals, dolls, fantasy creatures, food, plants, toys, and almost anything else for visitors to "adopt" and display on their own site, usually with a link back. Collection pages were often called toyboxes or adoption centers, and the pets themselves, sometimes cyberpets or virtual pets, might come with names, personalities, certificates, care instructions, or a tiny fictional habitat.

Mystery eggs and hatchables added surprise. An artist could give adopters code for an egg image hosted on the artist's own server, then weeks later replace that file with whatever had hatched. Nothing on the adopter's page changed; the same URL simply became a creature. There was no guarantee what you'd get. Basically a tiny web-graphics gachapon.

Whole sites built around clickable adoptables turned that into a game. You adopted an egg or pet, put its image and link in a forum signature or on your site, and other people's views or clicks helped it hatch, grow, get fed, gain experience, or level up. Dragon Cave[41] became one of the best-known examples; Valenth, Squiby, MagiStream, and PokéFarm ran their own variations. Forum signatures could end up full of little creatures begging to be clicked: the pet got fed and the adoption site got free advertising at the same time.[42]

People also made graphics specifically for each other. Gifts, freebies, trades, personalized site titles, dolls, buttons, and signatures were their own social activity. A site might keep a whole page of things friends or visitors had made for its owner, each graphic doubling as a record of who gave it to them.

The Quilting Bee (Q*Bee)[48], founded February 18, 2000 and active until 2014,[49] was an early club built specifically around trading pixel web graphics. Members collected patches and "sewed" them onto personal pixel quilts, creating a visual directory of who was making things at the time. Monthly pixel challenges sat beside blog-a-thons, story writing, holiday-card exchanges, and other activities; active members earned patches and pixel mushrooms for what they contributed.

Online TCGs ran on the same instinct, just with image cards instead of pixel patches. Senshi Card-Mania!, opened January 22, 2002, is generally credited as the first fan-run online TCG.[88] Members earned randomized cards through games and site activities, traded duplicates, and kept a trade post on their own website showing what they owned, what they were collecting, and what they had spare. Your website was your binder. The culture eventually got elaborate enough to produce dedicated trade-post layouts and management scripts.

Dollz and Pixel Communities

KiSS (Kisekae Set System) is a separate digital-doll lineage that actually predates The Palace. The KiSS/GS specification says the paper-doll program had been in development for NEC PC-9801VM2 computers since March 1991.[85] A KiSS set bundled the doll and clothing graphics into its own file format, then a dedicated viewer let you click and drag the clothes into place. It began in Japan and spread internationally through the 1990s, eventually gaining animation, sound, and other interactivity. It looks related in hindsight because both involve dressing little digital people, but KiSS wasn't part of the Palace Dollz format.

The web-doll culture later known as Dollz grew out of The Palace, the graphical chat client opened to the public in 1995.[87] Palace avatars were built from 44×44 graphics called props, with up to nine fitting inside a 132×132 area. Early Dollz commonly used three of those props stacked vertically for the body and the rest for clothing or accessories, layered on in the prop editor or drawn directly onto the base. Individual Palaces often kept rooms full of saved avatars sorted by type, so you might cycle through preps, sk8ers, silents, and whatever else that Palace had collected. Early names for the graphics themselves included lil people and paper dolls before "Dollz" took over as the general web-culture term. The drag-and-drop dollmakers most people picture now became widespread once dolling moved onto GeoCities, Angelfire, and other personal sites: a base plus swappable clothing, hair, and props, then saved, edited, traded, adopted, or entered into contests. Crediting the base artist mattered for the same reason crediting any graphics artist mattered. Dollz Mania and The Doll Palace became large anchors in a much wider network of smaller sites offering bases, makers, blinkies, tutorials, adoptables, requests, and contests.[43][44]

As dolling got more elaborate in the early 2000s, some artists drew whole bases, clothing, and hair themselves instead of assembling existing Palace-style pieces. Some communities preferred dolls or pixel dolls for that more art-driven practice, partly to separate it from simple dollmaker output and the earlier "dollz" fad. The line was never firm, and "Cartoon Dollz" floating around as yet another name didn't help. Mostly, "dolls" was just an awful search term: it could mean Barbies, porcelain collectibles, or paper crafts, while "cartoon dollz" got you much closer to the little illustrated people made for webpages. The subtypes told you more anyway: Preps, Sk8rs, Silents, Minis, Wonderkins, Divas, and plenty of others, each with a recognizable body type, pose, or base lineage.

Korean Dollz are a distinct branch, not another name for the Western hobby. They came out of Korean avatar portals and virtual-goods systems around the turn of the 2000s. SayClub was already selling digital avatar clothing by November 2000; Freechal, Daum, Cyworld, and others developed their own avatar economies around the same period. The production side was different too. Instead of hobbyists trading hand-drawn bases, commercial portal artists produced bodies, hairstyles, clothing, and accessories that users layered onto a character, often buying individual items as virtual goods. Cyworld's MiniMe went further by living inside a user's minihompy and decorated miniroom, a little stand-in for both the person and their space.

Candybar[45] was a South Korean casual-gaming and avatar portal developed by Neople. It opened in beta on 21 December 2001, became known for games including Love Love Studio and Kung Kung Tta, and closed on 7 September 2014. Korea had a much larger pixel-art scene around the same time, including games such as Ragnarok Online in 2002 and MapleStory in 2003.

Candybar became one of the Korean avatar systems most visible to English-language dolling sites. Its graphics grew especially popular in Western dolling around 2004 after they were repurposed into unofficial dollmakers, most famously by eLouai.[46] The avatars are usually easy to spot beside classic Palace dollz: denser shading, elaborate contemporary clothes, tiny accessories, glossy or glittery highlights, and stylized faces with comparatively huge heads. That polish came from a commercial avatar pipeline rather than the hobbyist practice of trading hand-drawn bases. Western sites hosted the graphics, separated out the parts, and folded them into Dollz culture. "Korean Dollz" seems to be mostly an English-language collector label; the original services called them avatars and avatar items. Beyond Candybar, the wider scene included Cyworld, SayClub, Netmarble, Mgame, Empas, Hangame, Toreads, UniAvatar, Daum, Buddy Buddy, Freechal, and Edupia. Much of it is difficult to reconstruct because the avatars were tied to user accounts and usually hidden behind a login, where the Wayback Machine could not reach them.

Beyond the Homepage: Forums, Messaging, and Profiles

This all branched out well beyond homepages, too. Forums, AIM, LiveJournal, and later MySpace ran alongside personal domains for years, and the same graphics culture moved between them constantly. Website buttons became profile badges and stamps. Guestbooks and comments coexisted. Doll and pixel-art communities showed up in avatars and icons as easily as on a homepage. Graphics sites added Photoshop brushes, textures, and templates beside the older GIFs and websets, serving whichever platform you happened to be decorating that week.

Forums offered two spots to customize and express yourself: an avatar beside your username, and a signature under your posts. They could match as a set, or be their own separate things entirely. Graphics boards often bundled requests for both together, handled by the same pool of designers.

Which format you actually needed depended on where you were, and plenty of people kept accounts (and icons) on several platforms at once. Forum avatars followed whatever limits the board imposed. AIM buddy icons ran 48×48 or 50×50, MSN Messenger display pictures were 96×96, and LiveJournal userpics were 100×100. Dedicated sites supplied all of it too, collecting AIM icons, MSN pictures, Yahoo smileys, and away messages by the thousands.

Signatures were the customizable block automatically attached beneath forum posts. They could be one line of text or a quote, but people often treated the space like a miniature profile: custom graphics, usernames, favorite characters, animations, links, whatever the board's limits allowed. Making signatures became its own branch of web graphics. You could make one yourself, ask a friend, or post in a request thread where a designer took your username, character or image, colors, text, and general direction, then posted the finished version back, sometimes with revisions or several options.

The graphic inside a forum signature was often called a sig tag. It usually centered on a character or other artwork, sometimes with a name or username worked into the design. People made them in programs such as Paint Shop Pro and Photoshop using swirl, grunge, sparkle, and vector brushes, along with pixel art, glitter, decorative type, and Paint Shop Pro tubes. You generally used one until you made or requested a new one to replace it.

Belle is archiving brushes from old graphics programs at Salvaged, including the kinds of assets used to make graphics like these.

By the mid-2000s, userbars had become another forum-signature staple: long, thin 350×19 banners, usually with a tiny image on one side and text on the other, announcing whatever the bar was about. Because they were only 19 pixels tall, you could stack several of them inside a forum's signature-height limit. They were also much easier to collect and swap around than a full custom signature graphic.

Stamps have a much cleaner origin. DeviantArt's then-creative director Ryan Ford created the format for the 2005 DeviantArt Summit, which was originally supposed to take place in an airplane hangar; the little air-mail or postage-style badge made sense for the event. The two earliest recovered examples were posted on April 22, 2005.[86] The standard became a 99×56 graphic framed by that recognizable perforated postage-stamp border, either static or animated.

Users almost immediately turned stamps into tiny visual bumper stickers for their identities and opinions. They covered fandoms, ships, software, music, games, political and social views, characters, aesthetics, jokes, and every variety of "I support…" or "Proud ___" declaration. Some had edgy Happy Bunny or Spencer's-tee energy; others were practical DeviantArt etiquette, telling visitors to view the full artwork, not steal it, leave feedback, stop repeatedly viewing a page without watching the account, or check whether requests and commissions were open.

The stamp was usually uploaded as a DeviantArt deviation in its own right. Other users didn't have to save the GIF and re-upload it just to display it. They could embed the original deviation with DeviantArt's thumbnail syntax, something like :thumb427548925:, so the stamp on their profile still pointed back to the creator's submission.

Iconator[50] collected huge libraries of AIM buddy icons, avatars, wallpapers, and away messages. Glitter-Graphics.com[51] and Dazzle Junction[52][53] supplied glitter text, backgrounds, layouts, and copy-and-paste code for MySpace and other profile sites. FlamingText[54] generated customized headings and animated text from whatever you typed into the box. The surface changed; the habit didn't.

You could take a background from one graphics site, a premade iframe layout from another teenager, learn how to change its scrollbar from Funky Chickens, grab an ALT character from CYP2, add an official Neopets GIF, put a Candybar doll beside the guestbook, and finish with a row of buttons linking back to everybody whose work you'd used. Of course it looked eclectic. Half the page came from different people.

The graphics did a lot of the social work themselves. A button sent you to somebody else's site; an award showed that another webmaster had approved of yours; a sig tag or blinkie said something about you without a paragraph of explanation. Even a tiled background could carry one person's work into hundreds of other people's websites.

Some formats really were defined by their dimensions, so the useful ones are worth keeping straight:

Format Common / canonical size Why this size?
Website button ("Link Me") 88×31 Popularized by early web badges, including Netscape's promotional buttons, and later recognized by the IAB as the "Micro Bar." The exact dimensions were a convention rather than a browser limitation.
Antipixel button 80×15 Established by Jeremy Hedley's Antipixel buttons in the early 2000s. The deliberately tiny format worked well for weblog sidebars and spread as a reusable template rather than because of a technical restriction.
Fanlisting code 50×50, 75×50, 100×35, 100×50 (sometimes 88×31 or 200×40) There was no single required size. Fanlistings offered several standardized code sizes so members could choose one that fit their own site's layout, and those dimensions became conventions across the community.
DeviantArt stamp 99×56 Originated with DeviantArt's 2005 Summit promotion as a tiny digital stamp or bumper sticker. The original 99×56 template was copied for user-made stamps until the dimensions became part of the format; it was not a DeviantArt image-size limit.
Userbar 350×19 Designed for forum signatures: wide enough for a label and small graphic, but short enough that several could be stacked without taking over a post. The exact 350×19 dimensions became a community template standard rather than a universal forum restriction.
Neocities-style banner blinkie 150×20 The classic blinkie template was long enough for a short line of animated text while remaining tiny enough to stack with other graphics. The dimensions were a community convention, not a Neocities restriction.
Web banner (banner exchange) 468×60 Standardized as the web advertising "full banner," allowing the same ad to circulate through banner exchanges and unrelated websites without being resized. It was later formalized as an IAB standard ad size.
AIM buddy icon 48×48 or 50×50 A client-imposed small icon size, roughly in line with contemporary desktop GUI icons and compact enough to sit inside a chat interface while keeping downloads small. AOL does not appear to have documented why it chose exactly 48–50 pixels.
MSN display picture 96×96 Messenger displayed and resized profile pictures to 96×96 as part of its interface design. The dimension also matches Windows' traditional 96-DPI baseline, although Microsoft does not appear to have documented that as the reason it chose 96 pixels.
LiveJournal userpic 100×100 A platform maximum suited to LiveJournal's text-heavy layout: large enough to work as an expressive image, but small enough to appear repeatedly beside posts and comments. The site's interface became heavily structured around the 100×100 limit, although the original reason for choosing exactly 100 pixels does not appear to be documented.
The Palace prop / maximum avatar area 44×44 / 132×132 The Palace was built around a fixed 44×44 sprite tile: the default roundhead and each wearable prop occupied one tile. An avatar could combine up to nine props across a 3×3 area, producing the 132×132 maximum. Small fixed graphics also suited the memory, bandwidth, and processing constraints of the time, although the reason 44 pixels specifically was chosen does not appear to be documented.

Blingee arrived after years of people already making, collecting, modifying, trading, and reusing small pieces of visual material online, and it didn't so much invent that behavior as pull it into one tool. Users uploaded their own stamps to the site, where anyone else could grab them and layer them into a collage of their own.

Blingee

Blingee[55] was a web-based platform dedicated to making glitter graphic collages; individual creations were commonly called Blingees. Launched in June 2006 as part of the Bauer Teen Network[56], the site offered an accessible browser-based editor that let users layer decorative "stamps," ranging from simple animated glitter and twinkling stars to complex image overlays, onto uploaded photographs. Bauer Publishing, which also owned teen magazine J-14, clearly understood their demographic.

Decorating personal photos with glittery, ornamental stamps had a parallel in physical youth culture too. In Japan, purikura[57] photo booths had been popular since the mid-1990s, letting users embellish their photos with cute digital decorations, sparkles, and frames. Purikura lived in physical arcades and Blingee lived purely online, but both came from the same impulse: maximalist self-expression through layered ornamentation.

Blingee fostered a social ecosystem where users formed groups, entered contests, and rated each other's creations. The platform clearly knew its audience, offering image dimensions optimized for MySpace profile pictures alongside built-in sharing to MySpace, Hi5[58], and Piczo[59]. Users could build personal collections and upload custom stamps to share freely with the community, keeping Web 1.0's collaborative ethos alive inside an increasingly centralized social web. And once a stamp was uploaded and used, it couldn't be taken back down off the site.

The platform faced a near-shutdown in August 2015, averted by widespread user outcry and emergency funding, after which previously premium features became free. Blingee ultimately closed for good in June 2024, unable to overcome the technical challenges left behind by Adobe Flash's deprecation in 2021.

The Web 2.0 Transition

The launch of Facebook[60] marked a shift in online social interaction. Unlike MySpace, which allowed extensive profile personalization, Facebook offered almost none. Around the same time, Web 2.0[61] pushed user-generated content and platform-driven participation, moving digital social spaces away from user-owned customization and toward content built inside centralized platforms. Mozilla Firefox[62] challenged Internet Explorer[63]'s dominance in a browser market that had gone stagnant, and Firefox's push for World Wide Web Consortium (W3C)[64] standards compliance moved web development away from "best viewed in IE" and toward cross-browser, standards-based design, one of the things that defined Web 2.0's interactive, platform-agnostic sites. The personal homepage, once central to online identity, started to feel antiquated as people migrated to centralized social networks.

Not every hosting service faded quietly, though: Lycos properties Tripod and Angelfire outlasted that shift by decades before finally going dark in 2026. A legal dispute in India left the Indian government in control of the assets of the company then holding Lycos, and by early January 2026 Angelfire's user-created sites had entered a prolonged outage, with the homepage itself reachable only sporadically. On March 6, 2026, a short-lived notice appeared on the Lycos homepage announcing that Angelfire and Tripod would shut down within 30 days and urging users to move their hosting elsewhere; the notice was pulled soon after but stayed visible in the page's source code, then reappeared on April 3.[65] Angelfire's homepage went offline for good on April 24, 2026, and Lycos dissolved the platform that same month, closing the book on nearly three decades of free web hosting.

Most free hosting services had already shuttered or gone paid by 2009, but glitter graphics didn't disappear. They migrated and persisted across MySpace, Tumblr[66], Facebook, and WhatsApp, where middle-aged and elderly users in particular picked up the habit of sharing image-based greetings. Circulated globally and often multilingual, these graphics mark mornings, holidays, birthdays, religious observances, and other everyday milestones, a lightweight but emotionally resonant way to keep up social bonds across distance. They commonly pair coffee cups, flowers, and decorative typography with animated glitter and sparkles, earnest affection dressed in visual excess.

The animated part, though, tends to get lost along the way. People often screenshot these graphics to reshare them faster instead of saving the original file, which immediately turns the animation into a static image. Cropping makes it worse, since a screenshot might catch phone or app UI at the edges, forcing another crop that eats into the graphic itself. Every re-upload as the image bounces between platforms puts it through another round of compression, so quality degrades a little more each time, a process everyone just calls "deep frying." What started out crisp and animated often ends its journey blurry, pixelated, and still, an artifact that's passed through countless hands.

Impact

Today they resurface in nostalgic internet communities, on platforms like Neocities[67] that deliberately embrace early web aesthetics, and linger in GIF keyboards like Tenor[68] and GIPHY[69], though many apps have since moved to newer alternatives like Klipy.

Some vocabulary drifted along the way, too. A modern Neocities guide will define a "shrine" as any enthusiastically curated page about something the webmaster loves — Pokémon, a book, a tabletop campaign, anything — which is noticeably broader than the character- and fandom-specific shrines of the GeoCities era, and has become close to a standard building block of personal-site vocabulary in the revival scene. Useful term, but it's revival-era codification of an old fandom habit, not a feature every GeoCities user actually had sitting next to their toybox page back in the day.

The look has also made its way into contemporary art and pop culture, particularly within Net Art[70] and other digital-culture movements that celebrate early web aesthetics. M.I.A.[71]'s 2010 single XXXO[72] references the Blingee style directly, its music video full of glittering floral graphics and decorative stamps straight out of the maximalist aesthetic.

A lot has also been lost. Resources have vanished as digital archives age and the personal hosting services that once carried them disappear, and even where an archive survives, the images it linked to often don't. The loss of external image hosts like Boomspeed[73], TinyPic[74], and Photobucket[75] stings the most, since limited bandwidth and storage on personal hosting meant images were often stored off-site rather than locally. Those platforms have since shut down, or in Photobucket's case, gotten bought out and turned into a paid service that extorts users by slapping giant watermarks on their images unless they pay up.

Technical Foundation

The GIF[76] format, introduced in 1987 by CompuServe[77], became the foundation for animated glitter graphics thanks to its support for multiple frames and near-universal browser compatibility. Steve Wilhite[78], the engineering lead on the team that built the format, insisted it's pronounced the same as Jif, sparking one of the internet's most heated debates. I pronounce GIF with a hard "G" and locked in that pronunciation by age six, so he should've spoken up sooner. May he rest in peace though.

Early glitter graphics were made with digital art programs like Adobe ImageReady[79] and JASC Paint Shop Pro[80]'s companion program Animation Shop[81], cheaper image editing options for entry-level designers. Sites like Blingee, PicMix[82], and Online Image Editor[83] made this style of graphic even easier to produce for free. They're all still around today, but the experience felt too clunky and outdated to enjoy, which is why I built Glitter Graphics. No shade.
