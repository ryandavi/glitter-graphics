History

Glitter Graphics are animated graphics characterized by looping sparkle and glitter effects applied to text, illustrations, or photographs.

They belong to the decorative, homemade visual culture of the personal web: maximalist[1] digital vernacular art, excessive, sincere, highly reusable, and eventually very far from the minimalism that took over mainstream web design. Their roots go back to the personal-website graphics culture of the 1990s and early 2000s. There are really two histories here. One is the broad practice of making animated sparkle graphics, which has no single inventor I can identify. The other is the provenance of one particular family of glitter tiles, the one preserved in this editor, and that family can now be traced to a small circle of WebTV graphics makers who were passing animations to one another by email in early 2001.

The format most people picture when they hear "glitter graphic" belongs to the customizable social-network profiles of the mid-to-late 2000s, MySpace above all. Blingee, the tool most closely associated with it, arrived in that later stage, not the original GeoCities-era personal web.

The personal-web culture glitter graphics grew out of has its own article, . For the broader preservation story and a chronology of the platforms that shaped the personal web, see .

Origins and Early Development

Fills existed before glitter did. A fill is a graphic meant to repeat, most often as a page background, and the same repeating images were called tiles or bars. Since the beginning of the personal web, plenty of people made their own, and plenty of glitter fills never had anything to do with the files in this editor. But the classic tile family preserved runs through a specific community of users that used WebTV.

WebTV Networks was founded in 1995 and launched its service in 1996: a set-top box, sold by Sony and Philips among others, that put the web and email on an ordinary television over a dial-up phone line. Microsoft bought the company in 1997 for about $425 million, later renamed it MSN TV, and kept the service running until 30 September 2013.[2][3] A WebTV box was not a general-purpose computer. You could browse sites, send email, and build a homepage, but you could not install Photoshop, Paint Shop Pro, Animation Shop, ImageReady, or any of the other desktop software graphics makers were using. So WebTV users built an elaborate graphics workflow out of browser tools, image processors running on somebody else's server, free webspace, and transloaders.

The Glitter Connection

The WebTV Circle

Moon (also known as Moondogee), Aylana, Dan (also known as DAN-411), and Mica (also known as Stardrops) shared a named community, and it lived on Usenet, not on WebTV itself. Aylana's own Tripod page, archived in April 2000, lists the "4-WebTV NewsGroup" (news:alt.discuss.4-webtv) among the several newsgroups she posted to, captioned "Not just a NewsGroup, but a Family ~ Making WebTV History ~ Overcoming Mendacity."[4] That capture shows the newsgroup, and Aylana's place in it, existing well before the early 2001 exchange described next. A separate site kept a birthday roster for the Club-4-WebbTv group, and in the 2020 capture I found it lists Mica, Aylana, Dan, and Moon among dozens of other WebTV handles.[5] The group's Usenet address was news:alt.discuss.clubs.public.webtv.news.club-4-webbtv.

The Early 2001 Emails

Moon, who ran the graphics site scri8e.com, belonged to a small WebTV graphics circle and later described "The original 'Glitter Connection' from early 2001. That began as emails, rapid firing between a small group of people." Moon says those exchanges resulted in the birth of three sites: Aylana's Bring On The Glitter, Dan's Sparkelies, and Moon's own Flashites.[6] That is Moon's stated origin for all three, and the events it describes come before every glitter tutorial I've found.

Mica's Tile

On the current Flashites page, under a small teal animation, Moon names who made one of the first tiles. "This is one of the very first glitter tiles Made by Mica and shared with myself Aylana Dan & a couple of others," the caption says. Aylana and Dan "made their first glitter tiles from this animated tile and a couple of other colors Mica had put into a open sub-directory for us at her www.WTV-Zone.com account."[6] WTV-Zone was a web host that offered space mainly to WebTV users, and a lot of this story lived there: Moon, Aylana, and Mica all had accounts on it, and so did several of the tutorial and request pages mentioned later in this article.

Mica did have a homepage, though the archives show it saying only this: "!~Hi How are you doing~????? Nice to see you~!! :»)) Buh Bye Now~!! :»)) !~Stardrops©2001~!!" The rest of her WTV-Zone directory was for sharing things with other people in her WebTV community.[7] The sub-directory Moon's caption describes survives in the archives as /BAR, and it held eight variations of the tile: the original SparkButton.gif, dated 7 June 2001, a square version sparkSquare.gif with the same date, Sparkbutton-goldish.gif on 8 June, Sparkbutton-gray.gif on 15 July, Sparkbutton-blue.gif and Sparkbutton-apinkish.gif on 4 October, Sparkbutton-DkGrey.gif on 6 October, and orangeGlit.gif on 28 October 2001.[7] I'm guessing the folder's name refers to either dividers or bars, another word for the repeating graphics used as fills, but the folder held a variety of graphics.

Mica's tile still loads on Moon's site as FB12.gif, and it also turned up a second time under another name. An August 2002 archive of Moon's Flashites page singles out a file called sparkbutton.gif as "That Famous Darned sparkbutton!" and tells visitors to "Note the date of file creation." The archived sparkbutton.gif and FB12.gif are the same file, byte for byte, so the file Moon called "That Famous Darned sparkbutton!" is the file Moon later captioned as Mica's "one of the very first glitter tiles." The date Moon wanted people to notice survives too, though not on the page itself: when the Wayback Machine captured sparkbutton.gif on 13 June 2002, the WTV-Zone server reported it as last modified on 7 June 2001. A server timestamp records when a file was last written to that server, not when somebody first drew it, so the tile is at least that old. It is the earliest server-side date I've found in this lineage, and Mica's own copy in /BAR, captured on 2 September 2001, carries the same date in its directory listing.[8]

Animation Inside Text

The earliest tutorial I've found for putting an animation inside text dates from September 2001, and it was written for WebTV. VegasGal's Animate Your Name, signed "Tutorial written by Vegasgal©2001" and captured on 6 September 2001, walked WebTV users through putting an animation inside their name.[9] You typed the name into a web-based banner generator, then took the result to Magick Studio, a browser front end for ImageMagick, and composited an animated bar into the letters. The animation in this capture is a swirling rainbow, not glitter. No desktop software was involved at all. WebTV users couldn't run Paint Shop Pro, so the editing happened on web servers and the finished GIF got transloaded to your own webspace. A transloader was another web service acting as a middleman: you gave it the URL of the file you wanted, plus the FTP address, account name, and password for your GeoCities, Tripod, or other webspace, and its server fetched a copy and uploaded it straight into your account.[10] A computer user could download a GIF to a hard drive and upload it again. A WebTV user skipped the local computer entirely. Glitter tiles could drop straight into the same kind of workflow.

Dan's Gemstones

Mica's /BAR folder holds a copy of Dan's original opal, dated 20 November 2001, which is the earliest dated evidence I've found for Dan's own tiles.[11] Two days later, by its own account, Dan's page came into being. That page, Sparkelies, survives in a June 2004 capture on Tripod, signed DAN-411, and its broken counter says people had been visiting "since it was created on 11-22-01." That is the page's own date, not the archive's.[12]

In Moon's later account, Dan "came out with the rich colors and names you still see today on his webpage. Being a Jeweler by trade his names are a bit unusual." Moon credits him with the opal tile and says he had his site "up and ready for use in about 24 hrs," and that Aylana "was right behind him with 'Bring On The Glitter.'" Moon's own Flashites came along around the same time, at a point Moon no longer remembered exactly, with tiles "more on the OdD and unusal side." The account was written years later and doesn't date any individual file, but its central chronology is supported by surviving files and pages from 2001 and early 2002.

Sparkelies lists eighteen tiles by stone: Amethyst, Aquamarine, Blue Topaz, Emerald, Garnet, Gold-1, Gold-2, Peridot, Platinum, Rhodolite, Ruby, Opal, Sapphire, Silver, Tanzanite, Tourmaline, Topaz, and Turquoise. I pulled the archived files and checked them against this editor's fills. Eleven are the same files byte for byte: amethyst, aquamarine, blue topaz, emerald, the first gold, rhodolite, ruby, sapphire, tanzanite, tourmaline, and turquoise. A few had lost their stone names somewhere along the way, so Dan's emerald is filed here as plain green, his first gold as dark gold, and his teal tourmaline as a second turquoise. His opal matches this editor's opal fill pixel for pixel. Garnet, the second gold, peridot, silver, and topaz are recolors of the same 61×29 tile that never made it into this collection, and platinum is a separate three-tone design.

FB12 is 61×29 pixels, three frames, 110 milliseconds a frame: exactly the canvas and timing of Dan's gemstone tile. Flip Dan's tile vertically and the sparkles line up with Mica's, frame for frame. His tile is what her soft, hundred-plus-color shading looks like knocked down to five flat tones and mirrored top to bottom. The stretched column and row along Dan's left and bottom edges are original to this file, not an artifact some later recolor introduced; they carry through into every one of the seventeen tiles built on this pattern.

Mica is the earliest maker I can currently put a name to in this lineage, but her FB12 tile is not the pattern that dominates this collection. That is Dan's gemstone family. Seventeen fills preserved here use the same 61×29 pixel pattern in different colors, and eleven of Dan's surviving gemstone files are byte-identical to fills in this editor. Mica's tile gives the lineage an early starting point. In the surviving material collected here, Dan's jewel-tone variation is the branch that multiplied. Nobody in that group is its single inventor.

Mica's Directory

Mica's WTV-Zone directory kept working as a shared resource long after the first glitter tiles, and what survives of it is mostly tools. It carried a TOOLS.html page in the main directory,[13] a /MUSIK folder of MIDI files,[14] and a section called /CROPCRAFTS, a name she coined because "most of them need You to crop the ones you want to use from the larger gif file."[15] She was still adding to the graphics folder in 2003: its newest file, Twinkle-Fairy.ttf, is dated 03 January 2004, and the last capture of the folder before returning a 404 error is from 26 February 2004.[7] She also hosted a project of games she had inherited from Jane, in /Janes, which the page says was last updated 6 January 2004 and which was still being captured in December 2004.[16] Other people used what she shared: katdr, on Tripod, thanks Stardrops for her tools on a page archived in June 2004.[17]

The Three Sites

By early 2002, other people's pages were naming Moon's, Aylana's, and Dan's sites directly. A newsgroup tutorial from Gypsy Jewels, dated 12 January 2002 and preserved on LadyWolfette's site, signs off "Thank You Moondoggee" and shows how to composite one of Moon's Flashites into just part of an image in ImageMagick, or several of them into separate parts.[18] By 23 February 2002, VegasGal's name tutorial had added Bring On The Glitter and Sparkelies to its list of animations.[9] By 6 March 2002, AnnaCher was running Names By Request on her WTV-Zone page: pick a color of glitter, say what you want the name or banner to read, allow time for the order, and transload the finished GIF quickly, because it would be deleted to make room for the next one.[19]

Aylana's Bring On The Glitter is the most extensive early repository from this network that survives. The oldest capture I found, of its main index on 6 March 2002, already sorts a large library by color and type: orange, gold, clear, blue, red, green, Christmas multi, aqua and seafoam, black, purple, neutral and beige, pink, white and silver, then Starspangled, Swirlycues, Velvet-Embers, a Molten Lava section flagged "EVEN NEWER," Crystalshards, Stardust, Just A Bit Of Glitz, GlitterLines, and Confetti. The index sends visitors on to "Moondogee and Dan-411" at Flashites and Sparkelies, and it recommends one tutorial per platform: VegasGal's animated-name tutorial, "Written By VegasGal©2001," under "Attention MSN (WebTv) Users," and Bemymind's "How To Glitter Your Fonts Tutorial written by Bemymind© January,2002" under "Attention AOL Users." It also invites people into the Club4WebbTv newsgroup, on Usenet, to show what they made with the glitter.[20] Asset library, tutorial, finished graphic, and a place to show it off, all linked from one page. Long before Blingee, the habits were already there: find the pieces, save them, make or request something from them, credit the maker, and put the result somewhere of your own. None of that makes Aylana the maker of every tile on it; by Moon's account some of the earliest material started with Mica.

I ran the same check against Aylana's archived files. Of the seventy-four sparkle fills bundled in this editor, at least forty-nine are identical to files on Bring On The Glitter, nearly all of them byte for byte, and that is only counting the pages I managed to pull out of the archive. They include most of the 60×30 family: the reds, oranges, crimsons, seafoams, browns, golds, and the gold fill that matches the February 2003 dingbat further down, which on Aylana's site was simply gold-02.gif. Add Dan's gemstones and at least fifty-five of the seventy-four fills in this editor can now be matched to identical surviving files on Glitter Connection sites. The rest may have come from the same places through pages that didn't survive, or from somewhere else entirely.

The site's full name was designbyAylana™, and Bring On The Glitter was one subsection of it. Its pages carry a copyright line reading ©1997,1998,1999,2000,2001,2002, and the line was never extended past 2002. That dates her graphic design in general, not the glitter library, which Moon places in 2001, but it suggests Aylana was making graphics as early as 1997.[20]

Bring On The Glitter took requests as well as offering tiles: made-to-order glitter names, like the ones AnnaCher was running. In December 2002, a notice from Aylana, signed "Aya," took over its front page and the library moved to a second index. The notice closed requests for glitter names and designs, and it stayed up, unchanged, into 2006.[21] The Wayback Machine returns errors for the site from November 2006 to August 2007, and its last successful capture is from 25 November 2009. By 28 December it returned a 404 again, and this time it never came back.[21]

WTV-Zone, the host where much of this story lived, described itself as "wholly owned and operated by the husband and wife team of Larry and Kari Bumeder and our son Kyle," running its own webservers out of the Pocono Mountains since March 2000.[22] A cascading hardware failure took the server down on 18 November 2022, "damaged beyond repair," and the closure, after "almost 22 years," went out ten days later in an email to subscribers, later shared by Mona Young in the Friends of Web TV and MSNTV Facebook group.[23] The wtv-zone.com domain itself kept resolving under different, unrelated ownership for a while after that, and finally stopped answering in June 2026.[22]

Making Glitter Graphics

By January 2002 there was a desktop version of the method using Paint Shop Pro. Bemymind's How To Glitter Your Fonts Tutorial, hosted by WebWench Graphics & Design, dated January 2002 and captured on 27 March, opens by trying to work out something already circulating: "The most fascinating thing i ever saw, was the glitter 'Happy New Year' gifs that i saw these past few weeks." The example shown was "not made by me, would love to know who did make it!" A friend, Ginger, "located the websites that had the glitter gifs to share," and the tutorial sends readers to Aylana's collection to choose one.[24] The method: open a three-frame glitter GIF in Animation Shop, copy each frame into Paint Shop Pro and save it separately, flood-fill the text or dingbat once with each frame as a pattern, then reassemble the three results in Animation Shop. "If you have a glitter with more frames, you will fill your font/ding equal to that many frames." The glitter the tutorial actually uses, a file called sea-05.gif, is 60×30 pixels, three frames at 110 milliseconds, and it is pixel for pixel the same as the dark green fill in this editor. So the 60×30 family was already circulating at the start of 2002, and already being handed to beginners as the thing to fill letters with. The tutorial is not an origin point. Its author says so in the first paragraph. It is also still online, on Bemymind's own bemymind.com.

A blank was a prepared graphic used as the base, or matte, for applying glitter or another fill, and almost anything could become one. Type a name in an ordinary font and turn the text itself into a blank; paint or draw a shape and use that; or take some other existing graphic and prepare selected areas of it, typically by making a color or range of colors transparent, so glitter could be composited in behind them. What made something a blank was its job in the workflow, not where the image came from, which is why the tutorials use the word for plain text graphics and pictorial ones alike.

Dingbat fonts were one source of shapes that could be turned into blanks, alongside ordinary text, painted shapes, and existing graphics. A dingbat font is a font built from pictorial symbols, ornaments, silhouettes, or other small images sitting at the positions normally used for letters and numbers, so pressing a key could give you a shamrock, a star, a skull, a zodiac sign, whatever the font happened to map there. Wingdings, released in 1992, and Webdings, released in 1997, are two of the best known.[25][26] Rendering one of those characters as a plain image and saving it was a fast way to get a blank without drawing a shape yourself, and a lot of surviving tutorials reach for dingbats specifically for that reason. The vocabulary stays loose here too: "dingbat" could mean the font, the character picked out of it, or the graphic rendered from that character. scri8e.com's Shamrocks page shows the dingbat case directly: "Most of the images from my Shamrocks index have been created by making a image from a php script. Then composited on dingbat Blanks, masks, models etc."[27] Masks controlled which parts of another image showed through; models isn't defined further here without more evidence of how the term was used.

The same site also kept a collection of downloadable dingbat and novelty TTF fonts behind a "No Peeking!" message and a reposted 2004 Usenet joke about "Fonters Disease," the particular frustration of a dingbat TTF whose useful symbols are hidden behind unpredictable keys[28], a reminder that fonts were as much a part of this toolkit as the finished tiles. WebTV made the blank-and-fill method especially practical, since its users had server-side tools and hosted assets instead of local art software, but the same idea carried straight into PC tutorials: prepare a blank from text, a dingbat, or some other shape, then fill or composite it, animate if needed, done in Paint Shop Pro and Animation Shop instead of a browser.

By early 2002 there were two toolchains for that shared method. On WebTV, you generated the text with banner generator, composited a hosted animated filler into it with ImageMagick, and transloaded the output. On the desktop, you downloaded the glitter, split its frames, used each one as a Paint Shop Pro pattern, flood-filled, and rebuilt the animation in Animation Shop. The same tiles fed both. StinkyWetDog's How To Make A Three Color Sparkle Font, captured 9 August 2002, shows how elaborate the WebTV workflow could get: generate the name, transload it, bring it into ImageMagick with three animated sparkle bars, composite silver across the letters, red into the top, and blue into the bottom, add borders if you like, output a looping multi-frame GIF, and transload it home.[29] The site's homepage was already offering visitors a way to "make your own custom fonts and frames" in a May 2002 capture.

An August 2002 Flashites page calls them Image Fillers, "I.F.'s" for short, and explains how to let the animation show through only part of a picture, a shoulder or whatever the mask left transparent.[30] The page demonstrates with a Hajime Sorayama chrome robot illustration, a fifteen-frame animation with a filler sparkling across her arm and shoulder, whose own server timestamp reads 13 July 2001. Its disabled counter says it had been counting visitors since January 7, 2002, though a counter's start date only says when the counter started, not necessarily when this page went up. Near the bottom, one asset is labeled "I.F. That Famous Darned sparkbutton!" with a marquee underneath telling visitors to "Note the date of file creation." The page never states that date, but the file itself is Mica's tile, and its server copy was dated 7 June 2001. By August 2002 a fourteen-month-old GIF was already famous inside this circle. By 2004, Moon's methods overview listed seven different ways to glitter or sparkle part of an image: cutting out and overlaying; compositing ATOP without tiling; ATOP tiling across one piece, like a Prep doll's dress; traditional animation with sparkles in different places on each frame; table stacking; ImageMagick's roll method; and "Morphimation," short for the Morph-Animated Method. The surviving example below layers sparkling roses with animated decorative effects, already reading as a kind of proto-Blingee aesthetic, though it would be a stretch to call it a direct precursor. The visual language of heavily decorated, sparkling imagery was around well before Blingee existed to formalize it. The same page warns that compositing a still image into an animated glitter gives the finished graphic the glitter's frame count, so a 28-frame tile could push a picture past what an ImageMagick site would output.[6]

By late 2002, tutorials were also teaching people to make their own glitter tiles instead of downloading them. Catarific's Glitter Fun, captured 5 October 2002, builds three slightly different sparkling textures with plug-in filters, animates them, and turns them into fills for "sparkly sig tags."[31][32] DreamerzDesigns' Paint Shop Pro 7 tutorial, captured eleven days later, starts from a gradient or solid color, duplicates it into three images, gives each different noise, stamps sparkle picture tubes over the top, and floods three copies of the text with the results; its downloadable fills had names like Sparkly Gold, Sparkly Purple, and Opal.[33] Whisper's EZ Animated Glitter Tiles, which says it went up on 26 February 2003, makes a 50×50 tile on three layers with a different amount of noise on each, and a tester page shows what other people made with it.[34] Sweet and Low's Simple Animated Glitter Text, captured May 2003, calls itself the author's version of an effect that already had "many different" versions online, and builds its three tiles from noise and Hoods sparkle tubes.[35][36] Three frames were common but never required: Ann's Glitter Pegasus Tutorial, captured February 2003, fills its Pegasus from four gold tiles and assembles four frames.[37] OnyxSmiles was handing out three teal glitter files with a glitter-name tutorial by August 2003, and by October ran a separate glitter collection sorted by color.[38] As far as I can match them, none of these use the tiles preserved in this editor. They reach the same effect by other routes, which is why glitter from this period varies so much in texture, size, frame count, and rhythm.

By 2004, glitter repositories were posting their own rules for using their tiles. Auburn Angel's Glitter Index, captured June 2004, sorted original tiles into holiday, colored, diamond, water, metallic, silver, and gold glitters, allowed them on signatures, dolls, personal sites, borders, and non-commercial web graphics, and forbade redistribution, reposting as a collection, alteration, and hotlinking.[39] A GeoCities glitter page by SMHart, with captures going back to June 2004, put the bandwidth problem in one line: "Please do NOT link directly to the glitters. It shuts the site down."[40] By August 2005, Alicorn's Adding Glitter to a Graphic on Upsited could list "a 3 frame glitter" among the materials, saved to your computer, note that glitters with more frames would work if you duplicated enough layers, and send readers to Bring On The Glitter to find one.[41] Four years after the Glitter Connection, the tutorial could simply assume the reader knew what "a 3 frame glitter" was and tell them to go get one.

The WebTV method was still being taught in 2006. Spider's Web Tutorials, a Neopets-era tutorial site, had a Sparkle Name tutorial, and the version captured in July 2006 repeats that method nearly step for step: make white text on a transparent background in banner generator, set "User type" to WebTV, composite a glitter over it in Magick Studio with Atop, tick "Tile across and down the image canvas," output it, and save it as a GIF, because "if you save it as a bmp, png, jpg or anything else it will not sparkle." For the glitter itself, it sent readers first to Bring On The Glitter, then to Sparkling Dots and The Vault.[42]

Following that page's glitter links leads back into the same network of sites, and to an earlier date. Sparkling Dots, on Angelfire, was captured on 21 June 2003, and it lists an Animated Name tutorial hosted on Tripod under 4sammy0.[43] That tutorial says "Page Created-December 1st, 2001 By-Becky (BW) from MsLauraLou's News Group" and adds "NOTE THE ORIGINAL CREATOR OF THE ANIMATED NAME IS: (~ KELLI FOX ~)." The only capture of it I found is from 25 December 2005, so the December 2001 date is the page's own, not the archive's, and Kelli Fox is a name that appears nowhere else in this story.[44] The tutorial also links BannerzRus's Powerlines, on WTV-Zone, as a resource for bars and fills, in a capture from 4 March 2005.[45] The other glitter source Spider's Web Tutorials names, The Vault, may be the page headed "The Vault #2, Inserts & Accents" at R&C Gif Manor, captured 15 October 2002; I am guessing that from the name. It calls its graphics inserts and accents: "Inserts and Accents are a fun thing, to add a lot of pizzazz, and total originality to your e-mail or site pages. They can be added to just about anything...names, banners, (transparent back) and to most dingbat style gifs and silhouettes. They enlarge very nicely for use as backgrounds."[46]

For the GIF format itself, frame timing, and the speeds the surviving fills actually run at, see How Glitter GIFs Worked.

Early Finished Graphics

The finished graphics, meanwhile, spread across personal sites. Glitter graphics circulated through online forum signatures, and surviving examples turn up on GeoCities[47], Angelfire[48], and Tripod[49] pages from the same period. Those three were part of a much larger personal-site ecosystem that had already given millions of people somewhere to publish their own pages, including AOL Hometown[50] and Homestead[51]. None of these hosts were primarily known for glitter graphics. GeoCities in particular already had a fully formed visual culture years before glitter showed up on it. People made glitter graphics, collected them from other creators, reposted them, and wrote tutorials showing other people how to make their own, and some of that activity happened on hosts that had been around since the 1990s. The surviving captures don't tell me how common glitter was across GeoCities as a whole, but by 2004 I stopped having much trouble finding it.

The clearest early finished example I've found on GeoCities is a page called all that Glitters, captured on 16 February 2003. The page exists to show off the owner's own animated GIFs. "I am learning to make my own gifs from dingbats," it says. "This is a sampling of some of my work," and the page is a surviving example of the blank-and-fill workflow described above, made from dingbats specifically, not the place where that workflow starts. One graphic is credited to a friend, Troy-Mich, who gave permission for it to be posted. Underneath the images is an instruction that turns up constantly in this culture: "PLEASE, download to your PC or transload to your own site as GeoCities DOES NOT allow linking!" Here the warning invokes GeoCities' own hosting rules rather than the creator's personal terms. The two options in that sentence map neatly onto the hardware split: computer users could download the GIF to a hard drive, while WebTV users could have a transloader copy it directly into their own webspace. The February 2003 capture is firm evidence that this page and its graphics existed by that date. A "© copyright 2002 here2eternity" notice runs along the bottom, which tells you what the owner claimed for the site, not that these particular GIFs were finished in 2002. It is no longer close to the beginning of this lineage. By then the Glitter Connection tiles had been circulating for about two years. The page earns its place because it shows a finished graphic and the fill that went into it side by side.

Whatever exact software steps here2eternity used, the finished files show the same basic construction Bemymind had documented a year earlier: take a small tile of animated glitter and use it to fill letterforms or a shape. For the dingbat, the fill that went into it survives too. Its gold glitter carries the same eight-color palette and two of the three frames found in a 60×30 fill preserved in this editor's asset collection, run as a shorter two-frame loop rather than reproducing all three. So both halves of the process reach us in one example: the finished February 2003 graphic and glitter matching what was poured into it.

The dingbat uses only two of the tile's three frames. These animations were assembled by hand in ImageReady, Paint Shop Pro, or Animation Shop, one frame at a time. Two alternating frames still read as a twinkling surface, and anybody turning out names, dingbats, and signatures in quantity had one less frame to place on every graphic. Moon's Flashites pages make the same practical point: two different frames are enough to animate, and every frame in the glitter becomes a frame in the finished file. Whether that is why the third frame is absent here is guesswork; the maker never said.

What the Files Reveal

Most of the seventy-four sparkle fills bundled here are recolors of a handful of tiles. Everything below describes this collection, the files I was able to compare, not every glitter tile anybody ever made. Sixty-seven are flat tiles built from a few solid tones, and those sixty-seven reduce to about twenty distinct pixel patterns. One 61×29 tile accounts for seventeen of them on its own.

60×30 2 2 black, light purple

Almost every flat fill in this collection follows the same recipe: two to five solid tones plus one pure white sparkle, with the white taking up twelve or thirteen percent of the pixels every single time, whatever the tile and whatever the color. Fifty-three of the flat fills use five tones in almost exactly the same proportions. The plainer ones are not a different idea; they are the same tiles with the shading knocked out. Flatten the four base tones of the orange fill into one solid color and keep the sparkle, and you have the black fill exactly, pixel for pixel. That is why black and light purple read as white glitter scattered over a flat background: that is precisely what they are.

The other seven fills, including the rainbow, camo, patriotic, light blue, pastel blue, and burn sienna tiles, are dithered instead of flat. They run from twelve colors up to two hundred and twenty-six, and they come from some other tradition of making these things.

Both sizes and all these variants are in the editor because I keep whichever surviving version I could actually find rather than quietly cleaning every old fill into one standardized copy. Most of them repeat a column down the left edge, some repeat the top row as well, and that doubled line shows wherever the tile starts over. It is part of what survives.

From WebTV to the Wider Web

Net artist Olia Lialina singled out another example while cataloguing the platform's visual conventions: a page in the Heartland/Prairie neighborhood that stamps itself "Last Updated: Thursday, August 5, 2004" and carries a Bible, a rose, and a layer of animated sparkle. She was struck by how out of place it felt: "Glitter is Myspace, not Geocities. Geocities are 90es, not 00s."[52] The graphic itself wasn't early: the surviving GeoCities examples go back at least a year and a half further, and the tiles themselves more than three. Her reaction is worth keeping for the mismatch in it. Glitter was already circulating on GeoCities, and she still read it as belonging to the newer social-web aesthetic that would become much more visible on MySpace.

By the mid-2000s, whole websites existed just to make or hand out glitter graphics. Generators, signature makers, and graphics collections ran as destinations of their own, and the filenames, links, and source pages of surviving glitter assets point back to Glitterfy, GlitterYourWay, 123Glitter, GlitterMaker, GlitterKiss, GlitzHeaven, NicolsHotel, GlitterTextGraphics, and Cute-Spot, among others. Most of those domains are now parked or repurposed; the list of what survives is in Resources.

As glitter spread beyond the early WebTV circle, it was applied to almost anything: Disney and cartoon characters, celebrities and bands, dolls, anime, flowers, butterflies, angels, zodiac signs, fantasy art, photographs, holiday graphics, romantic messages, and a lot of hearts. Glitter graphics were also strongly associated with feminine web culture, particularly the highly decorated personal pages, profiles, dolls, friendship graphics, and romantic imagery common on sites used by girls and young women. Glitter also turned up in emo and scene[53], goth imagery, and what would later be described as McBling[54]. Many early examples share the same basic construction: a short sparkle animation, often reused in different colors, laid over text or an existing image. They served plenty of specific jobs too: a name or username spelled out in glitter, a "thanks for the add" graphic for a new MySpace friend, a birthday or holiday greeting, a romantic message, a comment left on someone else's profile, a forum signature, a profile picture, a personalized photo. Names in particular echo older sig-tag and custom-graphic culture: asking someone to make you a glitter name graphic wasn't so different from asking a forum designer for a sig tag.

From Homepages to Profiles

Forums, AIM, LiveJournal, and later MySpace ran alongside personal domains for years. Website buttons became profile badges and stamps. Guestbooks and comments coexisted. Doll and pixel-art communities appeared in avatars and icons as easily as on a homepage. Graphics sites added Photoshop brushes, textures, and templates beside the older GIFs and websets, serving whichever platform you happened to be decorating that week.

The move from personal homepages to larger online communities did not immediately end page customization. Many still gave users considerable control over backgrounds, fonts, images, and layouts through HTML, CSS, or built-in customization tools. Glitter graphics moved with them. They appeared throughout Neopets[55] petpages, userlookups, and guilds, as well as on Xanga blogs and Piczo pages, and later across Gaia Online[56] and MySpace[57] profiles.

Standalone homepages lasted well after profiles became popular. For a while, the graphics culture just moved with its users onto whatever surface they logged into next. Writing in 2007, Lialina described people moving out of standalone homepages into "the language of MySpace" (accounts, profiles, journals, channels), and pointed out how much those new pages still resembled their Web 1.0 predecessors: a background, blocks of graphics, text, and video laid over it, held together by the same modular, HTML-based aesthetic. She specifically named glitter graphics as the look that had come to define this new amateur web, in contrast to the starry backgrounds of the 1990s homepage.[58] MySpace centralized where people lived online well before it centralized what their pages looked like.

Between personal websites and standardized social media came a middle stage of customizable, centralized profiles, and MySpace is the important transitional case. It still let users control how their own page looked, which made it an extension of the homepage habit more than a break from it. Glitter graphics flourished specifically in that window, while centralized platforms still gave users somewhere to put decorative external images, custom backgrounds, HTML, layouts, comments, and profile pictures.

Blingee arrived after years of people making, collecting, modifying, trading, and reusing small pieces of visual material online, the culture described in . It did not invent that behavior; it pulled the process into one tool. Earlier, building a personal page out of reusable pieces meant assembling a whole website: a tiled background from one creator, buttons from another, a doll from somewhere else, a blinkie, some copied HTML, and your own text and photos. Blingee moved that same modular habit inside a single image. Instead of a website, you assembled a picture out of glitter animations, borders, text, stars, hearts, characters, decorative overlays, a photograph, and stamps other members had uploaded.

Blingee

Blingee[59] was a web platform for making glitter collages; individual creations were called Blingees. Launched in 2006 as part of the Bauer Teen Network[60], its browser-based editor let users layer decorative "stamps" (from animated glitter and twinkling stars to complex image overlays) onto uploaded photographs. Bauer Publishing also owned the teen magazine J-14, so it clearly understood the demographic.

Decorating personal photos with glittery stamps had a physical parallel. In Japan, purikura[61] booths had been popular since the mid-1990s, letting users embellish their photos with cute decorations, sparkles, and frames. Purikura lived in arcades and Blingee lived online, but both offered maximalist self-expression through layered ornamentation.

Blingee built a community around groups, contests, and ratings. It offered image dimensions optimized for MySpace profile pictures alongside built-in sharing to MySpace, Hi5[62], and Piczo[63]. Blingee wasn't only a destination in itself. Its export sizes and sharing buttons were designed around the profile sites where its users were already posting and talking to each other.

A Blingee stamp worked on the same basic idea as older web graphics: one person uploads something, and other people use it in their own work. Earlier creators set their own terms for that kind of reuse: credit required, no alterations, no redistribution outside the original webset. Blingee handled it through the platform itself instead. Once a user uploaded a stamp, any other member could use it in their own Blingees, and once somebody actually had, the uploader could no longer delete it.[64][65] Users built up personal collections of stamps this way, and those collections became parts of other members' Blingees whether or not the original uploader kept using the site.

In September 2015, Lialina interviewed Irina Kuleshova, an orthodontist in Rostov-on-Don, in southern Russia, who made Blingees under the username ivk and had uploaded 4,559 stamps of her own.[65] She learned animation from downloaded tutorials and an unlicensed copy of Photoshop somebody gave her, and she worked out how other people's stamps were built by making a Blingee with one, saving the result, and pulling the file apart in Photoshop, because that was the only way to get a stamp off the site. A snow stamp took her two years of giving up and coming back to it. Blingee counted how often each stamp was used and handed out badges at a hundred, a thousand, ten thousand, and a hundred thousand uses, which is part of why copied stamps stung so much: whoever re-uploaded yours collected the points for it. "I am a free woman now," she told Lialina. "I married Blingee."

Blingee users made graphics in almost every style. The same editor could turn out a name spelled in glitter and hearts, a "Happy Birthday" card built from roses and animated text, an emo collage of skulls and broken hearts, a devotional image of Jesus surrounded by doves, or a fan edit of Avril Lavigne, and none of them would have looked out of place browsing the site's galleries. A huge share of what people made was personal and social: usernames and first names decorated with stars, flowers, hearts, or a favorite cartoon character, birthday and Christmas cards, good-morning and good-night graphics, and "I love you" or "best friends forever" pieces built around a bouquet, a teddy bear, or a coffee cup with a heart floating over the steam.

Blingees also reflected whatever subcultures and fandoms their makers belonged to on MySpace and similar sites. Scene and emo Blingees ran on favorite bands, skulls, broken hearts, and dark illustrations laid over a black-and-pink profile piece. McBling compositions loaded the same tools with rhinestones, metallics, designer handbags, flip phones, and celebrity faces. Hip-hop graphics wrapped a rapper's photo in money, jewelry, and flames. Fandom cut across all of it: anime and manga characters were common enough to form a visual culture of their own, and just as often bled into emo, scene, kawaii, or gothic treatments of the same image. Britney Spears, Avril Lavigne, Justin Bieber, Selena Gomez, Miley Cyrus, and the Jonas Brothers all turn up in surviving Blingees, alongside Disney Channel, iCarly, High School Musical, Twilight, Barbie, and Hello Kitty.

Fantasy Blingees put fairies, pixies, elves, and unicorns through the same glitter treatment as darker gothic work built from vampires, witches, and haunted forests. Religious users made their own devotional graphics: Jesus, saints, popes, crosses, and white doves layered with flowers and animated sparkle exactly like a cartoon character's dialogue bubble. Patriotic graphics did the same with flags, eagles, heraldic symbols, and national colors, and not only for the United States. A grandmother's birthday greeting, a glittering Jesus, and an emo broken-heart collage were all made with the same stamps and effects, and there's no reason to read any of them as a joke.

The platform nearly shut down in August 2015. User outcry followed, new funding kept it online, and previously premium features became free. Blingee finally closed in June 2024. The surviving closure notice did not give a reason, so attributing the shutdown to one specific cause would be guesswork.

Glitter After the Homepage

As the window for decorating profiles narrowed and major free hosts closed or moved to paid plans, glitter graphics migrated instead of disappearing. They persisted across MySpace, Tumblr[66], Facebook, and WhatsApp, where image-based greetings became a daily ritual for many users. Circulated globally and often multilingual, people send them to say good morning, mark birthdays and holidays, send religious greetings, tell somebody they're thinking of them, and generally keep in touch with friends and relatives. They pair coffee cups, flowers, and decorative typography with animated glitter and sparkles.

The animation tends to get lost along the way. Screenshooting a graphic instead of saving the original file immediately turns it into a static image. A screenshot may catch phone or app UI at the edges, and each crop eats farther into the graphic. Repeated downloads and re-uploads add compression artifacts until something that began crisp and animated ends up blurry, pixelated, and still.

Impact

Today Blingees resurface in nostalgic internet communities and on hosts such as Neocities[67], where people deliberately revive early web aesthetics. Glitter graphics also survive in searchable GIF libraries such as Tenor[68] and GIPHY[69]. When Google closed Tenor's public API in June 2026, some third-party apps moved to GIPHY or newer services such as Klipy.

Lialina made a point about the wider early web that applies here: collecting old MIDI files and animated GIFs preserves the artifacts, but not necessarily what problems they solved, how people actually used them, or which communities they belonged to.[70] That's part of why exists alongside this page: linkware, dolling, websets, fanlistings, adoptables, signatures, and MySpace layouts are what a glitter graphic was made to sit among, and the images tend to survive better than the practices that surrounded them.

The names of the people who made these graphics got lost the same way. Mica has no surviving homepage, and her directory on WTV-Zone is under the username Stardrops. The tile spread much further than either name did.

Glitter graphics sit comfortably inside both camp[71] and kitsch[72], the two words critics reach for when something is sincere and far too much at the same time. Both are useful for analyzing the work afterward, but neither is necessary to explain why somebody made one. Nobody needed a theory of camp to pour gold glitter into a dingbat. Most people were doing it because they liked how it looked.

The look has made its way into contemporary art and pop culture, especially Net Art[73] and work that deliberately revives early web aesthetics. The video for M.I.A.[74]'s 2010 single XXXO[75] draws heavily on the visual language associated with MySpace-era glitter graphics and Blingee, through glittering floral graphics and decorative stamps.

Resources vanish as archives age and the personal hosts that carried them disappear; even when a page survives, its externally hosted images often do not. The loss or restriction of image hosts such as Boomspeed[76], TinyPic[77], and Photobucket[78] stings because limited storage and bandwidth led many site owners to keep images off-site. TinyPic shut down; Photobucket survives as a paid service, but its policy changes replaced countless old embeds with giant watermarks or upgrade notices. The HTML remained while the page's face disappeared.

How Glitter GIFs Worked

The GIF[79] format, introduced in 1987 by CompuServe[80], could store multiple images from the beginning. The 1989 revision added timing and disposal controls that made browser animation practical. Combined with broad browser support, those features made GIF the foundation of animated glitter graphics. The spec stores that timing per frame, in hundredths of a second, which is why glitter speeds land on oddly specific numbers. Steve Wilhite[81] insisted the name is pronounced like Jif, sparking one of the internet's most heated debates. I pronounce GIF with a hard "G" and locked in that pronunciation by age six, so he should've spoken up sooner. May he rest in peace though.

The step-by-step methods are back in Making Glitter Graphics. The earliest documented method didn't use desktop software at all. It started on WebTV, where people typed their text into banner generator, composited an animated tile into it through a web front end to ImageMagick such as Magick Studio, and transloaded the finished GIF to their own webspace. On the desktop, the same job fell to Adobe ImageReady[82] or Jasc Paint Shop Pro[83] with its companion GIF program, Animation Shop,[84] and the earliest desktop tutorial I've found, Bemymind's, turns up about four months after the WebTV one. Later, Blingee, PicMix[85], and Online Image Editor[86] let people make similar graphics in a browser. PicMix and Online Image Editor remain available; Blingee closed in 2024. The surviving tools felt too clunky and outdated for me to enjoy, which is why I built Glitter Graphics. No shade.

Most glitter fills run at nearly the same speed. Sixty-two of the seventy-four sparkle fills bundled here hold each frame for 110 milliseconds, roughly nine frames a second, and four more run at 100. That is slow for animation, and it has to be. Three frames at that speed read as a surface catching the light; run the same three frames much faster and the glitter stops twinkling and starts to crawl.

Seven of the fills ask for no delay at all, a plain zero in the timing field. Browsers have never taken that literally. Anything under two hundredths of a second gets clamped, historically to 100 milliseconds, so those files play at about the same speed as everything else no matter what they request. Whether the zero meant "as fast as possible" or was just a field nobody filled in, the browser decided for them. One outlier, a dithered pastel blue tile, asks for 20 milliseconds across five frames and actually gets it.
