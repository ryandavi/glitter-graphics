Preservation

The personal web was built across thousands of services, tools, communities, and image hosts. This page is about what happened as that infrastructure changed or disappeared, and the people and projects that kept parts of it alive.

Why Preservation Matters

In the 1990s, being online often meant making a place of your own, partly because there wasn't much else on the internet yet. If you wanted a homepage, a profile, a collection of things you liked, or some kind of social space, you had to build it yourself. People made pages, decorated them, made graphics, linked to friends, joined webrings and communities, and invented their own ways of interacting. A lot of what websites now handle automatically had to be put together by hand.

For a while in the 2000s, all these different versions of the web existed at once: personal websites, blogs, instant messengers, forums, virtual worlds, art communities, customizable profiles, image hosts, bookmarking sites, and social networks. There wasn't one standard way a social website was supposed to work yet.

That started to change as more of the web moved onto bigger platforms. The social networks of the 2000s still left room for customization, but more of the structure came pre-built. You didn't need to make a page of friends when the site could generate a friends list for you, or maintain a links page when it could organize your connections. More and more of the work shifted from building the structure yourself to deciding what to put inside a structure someone else had already made. Eventually much of the social web settled into the same basic structure: profiles, feeds, likes, comments, and follows.

That doesn't mean people stopped making things online, just that the places where they could shape how they appeared became much more limited. Personal websites had often relied on image hosts too, especially when storage or bandwidth was limited, but those images could still be arranged into a page you designed yourself. On larger platforms, customization increasingly meant choosing a profile picture, adding a header or background, and deciding what to post. The surrounding layout belonged to the platform and looked essentially the same for everyone. The parts you could customize became smaller islands inside a design you no longer controlled.

The web people built by hand was easy to break. A personal site might be hosted in one place, pull its images from another, depend on Flash or some other plugin, embed a chatbox from another service, use an off-site hit counter, and belong to a community hosted somewhere else entirely. A lot of what looked like one website was actually assembled from pieces provided by several different services. If one piece disappeared, the page might still load, but part of what it was meant to show or do was gone. Enough missing pieces could strip away its design, functionality, and even some of its information while leaving the basic HTML behind. Image hosting makes this especially obvious: huge parts of the old web survive as HTML, while the externally hosted graphics that actually made up those pages are gone.

Many of those dependencies were controlled by companies the people using them had no say over. Services got bought, shut down, or changed their business model, sometimes with consequences far beyond the service itself. Polyvore ran for more than a decade before its site disappeared the same day SSENSE announced it had acquired Polyvore's assets. Yahoo stripped the web-accessible content out of Yahoo Groups before shutting the service down completely. Photobucket put third-party image hosting behind a paid plan and instantly broke images across forums, blogs, shops, and websites it didn't even own.

The old infrastructure disappeared unevenly. A lot of services from the personal-homepage era shut down in the late 2000s and early 2010s, while others hung around much longer. AOL dial-up lasted until 2025. In 2026, Lycos announced the shutdown of Angelfire and Tripod, and both eventually went dark. Some of these services outlived the version of the web they came from by decades.

The ability to make a website never disappeared with them. What disappeared was much of the easy entry point. Free hosts had given people somewhere to experiment without first paying for anything or understanding domains, hosting plans, servers, or how any of that infrastructure worked. You could decide you wanted a website and start making one. Once more of those services disappeared, building a site was still completely possible, but the first step became a little harder, especially for someone who wasn't yet sure they wanted to spend money on a hobby.

The instability of the web was obvious enough that even ordinary tools were built around it. Google's cached results could provide a copy when the original page or server was unavailable. When Google removed the feature in 2024, it said it had been created for a time when pages were much less reliable and that the web had since improved. That change also reflects a more centralized web, where much of what people encounter now lives on a smaller number of large, professionally maintained platforms rather than being scattered across independent hosts and services.

People have been trying to keep the web from disappearing for almost as long as it has existed. The Internet Archive was founded in 1996, and the Wayback Machine went public in 2001. Other projects approached the same problem in their own ways. Neocities gave people an easy platform to build personal websites again, while the Internet Archive's GifCities made graphics recovered from GeoCities searchable. The Palace survived in a more literal sense: Communities.com stopped officially supporting it and later went bankrupt, but users kept running their own Palace communities without them.

That gets harder when so much material is concentrated inside a few large platforms and publishers, because they can decide whether anyone is allowed to archive it. In 2025, Reddit restricted the Internet Archive from crawling most posts, comments, and profiles, saying AI companies had been scraping Reddit content through the Wayback Machine and raising concerns about deleted material remaining available there. Major news publishers have begun restricting the Archive too, stating that the concern has largely been that archived copies provide another route for AI companies to access copyrighted journalism, but those archives also preserve articles before they are edited, removed, redesigned, or placed behind different access restrictions. Without them, it becomes much harder to see what a page said at a particular point in time. A web concentrated in fewer places may be more technically reliable, but it also gives a relatively small number of companies much more control over what survives as part of its historical record.

Preserving old websites has become a culture of its own. Websites, graphics, profiles, forum posts, and entire communities are now old enough to count as historical artifacts, and a lot of the originals are already gone. Much of what's left survives only because somebody saved a file, archived a page, mirrored a site, or kept something running after the company behind it stopped caring.
