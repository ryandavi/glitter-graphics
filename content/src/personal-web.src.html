The Personal Web Aesthetic

The personal web of the {date:1990|1990s} and early {date:2000|2000s} was built out of other people's parts: tiled backgrounds, animated dividers, buttons, cliques, {tip:Graphics, often pets, dolls, or fantasy creatures, offered for visitors to save and display on their own websites, usually with credit or a link back}adoptables{/tip}, dolls, and the pages made to hold them. A lot of the socializing happened through those parts. A button sent you to somebody else's site; an award showed that another webmaster had approved of yours; a sig tag or blinkie said something about you without a paragraph of explanation. Even a tiled background could carry one person's work into hundreds of other people's websites.

Net artist {@olia-lialina} documented this culture in {y:2005}, when its conventions were still recent and much of the material was still online, even though the classic amateur homepage was already receding from the mainstream web. In {link:external|http://art.teleportacia.org/observation/vernacular/}A Vernacular Web{/link} she described a bright, cluttered, homemade web assembled by amateurs out of animated GIFs, tiled backgrounds, {tip:A compact music-file format commonly used for background music on early personal websites}MIDI files{/tip}, construction signs, and whatever else was lying around.[@lialina-olia-a-vernacular-web] People got used to encountering graphics as separate, reusable pieces of material, to be found, saved, collected, arranged, credited, traded, and, when the creator allowed it, edited or recombined ({goto:PersonalWebGraphicsWebsetsLinkware}Linkware{/goto} covers how possessive some of that culture actually was).

Glitter graphics were one piece of it, and their own story is in {open:historyModal}History{/open}. Most of this culture predates glitter or grew up alongside it, but it is the visual and social ground glitter graphics grew out of, and the ground {@blingee} later drew on more directly.

For the broader preservation story and a chronology of the platforms that shaped the personal web, see {open:preservationModal}Preservation{/open}.

What a Personal Site Was

Personal sites ranged from carefully designed to complete chaos. Plenty of people had real taste and a genuine eye for design, and a personal site was often where that eye got trained, one bad color choice and one too many blinking GIFs at a time. Maximalism was always available, though, and nobody was there to stop you: tiled backgrounds fighting five different fonts, animated glitter piled on more animated glitter, a MIDI file autoplaying whether you asked for it or not. Why wouldn't you post every pretty thing you could find? Personal websites weren't designed by a studio or approved by committee, so a site could be beautifully put together, a complete mess, or both at once depending on which page you clicked into.

Most of the time, you had no idea who was visiting your website. There was something slightly voyeuristic about it: someone could find it through a search engine, a {tip:A group of related websites connected in a navigable ring with Previous, Next, and Random links}webring{/tip}, another person's links page, or by clicking around for hours, read everything you had written, and leave without saying a word. Visitor-tracking services such as NedStat, eXTReMe Tracking, and later StatCounter could tell you how many visits you were getting, where people had come from, and sometimes what country or region they were in, but the visitors themselves were still mostly anonymous.

Hit counters made at least one part of that audience visible. A little number at the bottom of the page told you roughly how many times the site had been loaded, and because the counter was usually public, it told everybody else too. A high number could make a site look established or popular in much the same way a follower count does now. Guestbook entries, {tip:Sites that ranked participating websites, usually according to visitor votes or traffic sent through ranking buttons}topsite rankings{/tip}, and banner clicks offered a few more signs that somebody was actually out there, but none of them told you exactly who was reading.

Search engines increasingly influenced which sites were easiest to find, and {@google} in particular rewarded pages that other sites linked to. But there was no single feed deciding what everyone saw. People found personal sites through search engines, links pages, directories, webrings, topsites, forums, recommendations, and simply following links from one site to another. There was no platform template forcing every page into the same shape either. One person decided, in {@notepad}, a {tip:A visual editor whose output resembles the finished page; short for 'what you see is what you get'}WYSIWYG{/tip} editor, or whatever HTML box their host provided, what their little corner of the internet should look like. Some even wrote a whole "why I made this site" manifesto, sometimes running to genuine essay length, about something they'd built simply because they wanted to. I did it for this site!

Some pages existed for one specific obsession. A character shrine could be a single dedicated page within a larger personal site, or an entire mini-site of its own, devoted to one character, pairing, series, or other favorite subject. Depending on the corner of the web, the same thing might be called a fan page, fansite, tribute, dedication, or character page instead. Fan pages were widespread by the mid-{date:1990|1990s}, and "shrine" circulated in some fandom communities too; it just wasn't a universal personal-web term.

Shrines became especially common in anime and manga fandom. Before anime became much easier to find in the West, the internet was one of the places fans went to find information about series that might never appear on local television or in an ordinary video store. A fan site could have character profiles, episode summaries, image galleries, translations, fan art, fanfiction, and links to {tip:Fan-made translations and subtitled releases of media that had not necessarily received an official local-language release}fansubs{/tip} or other sites devoted to the same series. Sometimes you had to already be online to discover any of this in the first place. One anime page led to another, then to a webring or directory, and suddenly there was an entire fandom for something you might never have encountered offline.

"Shrine" has since taken on a broader meaning. Modern {@neocities} guides may define a "shrine" as any enthusiastically curated page about something the webmaster loves: {@pokemon}, a book, a tabletop campaign, anything. That meaning is broader than the character- and fandom-specific shrines found on older personal sites, and it has become a standard part of the revival scene's vocabulary. Useful term, but it is a revival-era codification of an old fandom habit, not a label every personal-site owner would have recognized or used at the time.

Making and Credit

Building a Personal Website

{@geocities} was the best-known and one of the largest free homepage hosts, but it was only one part of a much larger ecosystem. {@tripod}, {@angelfire}, {@homestead}, {@aol-hometown}, and countless smaller services also gave ordinary users free space to build and publish personal websites. What set {@geocities} apart was how thoroughly it turned that hosting space into a metaphorical place. Rather than simply placing users beneath account names or directory categories, early {@geocities} sites were assigned numbered addresses inside themed neighborhoods and suburbs. Its users were {tip:GeoCities' term for members who created personal homepages on the service}homesteaders{/tip}, living in places such as Hollywood, Area51, Tokyo, and WestHollywood. That system was phased out for new members in {y:1999}, when {@geocities} moved toward simpler username-based addresses. Archivists saved so much of it after {@yahoo} shut it down in {y:2009} that "{@geocities}" became shorthand for a whole style of amateur web design; the rescue is part of {open:preservationModal}Preservation{/open}.[@deleted-city][@1995-four-datasets]

Free homepage hosts organized their users in different ways, and sometimes that organization became part of the site's address. {@angelfire}, for example, originally required new members to choose from a set of predefined directories, producing addresses such as angelfire.com/realm2/username/ or angelfire.com/biz6/username/. These were not necessarily separate physical servers, but part of {@angelfire}'s own system for dividing up its hosted sites.[@angelfire-web-publishing-bootcamp] The requirement was eventually dropped for new accounts in favor of simpler subdomain addresses such as username.angelfire.com, while older sites kept their original directory-based URLs.[@angelfire-subdomain-signup] Other hosts developed their own systems. {@geocities} made its hierarchy part of the service's identity, placing homesteaders at numbered addresses inside themed neighborhoods and suburbs, while {@fortunecity} used a similar city metaphor with areas, streets, and numbered sites.[@1995-four-datasets][@fortunecity-archiveteam] What can look today like a strange extra piece of an old URL could therefore tell you something about the host that the site belonged to and, in some cases, where that host had placed it.

Owners of paid domains often gave other people space on them, and getting that space usually meant applying for it. A domain owner, often called a {tip:A domain owner who provided webspace or subdomains to other site owners}hostess{/tip}, gave selected {tip:A person given web space or a subdomain on somebody else's privately owned domain}hostees{/tip} a subdomain, an FTP account, and a small allowance of space and bandwidth, sometimes with MySQL, PHP, CGI, and script installation on top. It generally cost nothing beyond a credit or a link back, but applicants could be judged on their HTML and graphics work, their previous sites, their technical knowledge, and whether the hostess wanted that kind of content on her domain at all. {@kawaiiness} said in {y:2004} that it was looking for "cute, pixel or visitor based content/cliques/adoption sites," and asked for examples of previous work, FTP and hosting knowledge, and readable English.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] Having a good .com that other people wanted attached to their name could become a small status symbol, and being hosted on one worked much the same way.

{link:external|http://misa.phoophie.com/home.html}{@misa}'s site{/link}, still sitting on a {@phoophie} subdomain, is a surviving example of what that could look like. Its red custom scrollbars, custom cursor, and 7pt Georgia still load, and the navigation panels divide neatly into Link In, a row of buttons visitors were told to save to their own servers, and Link Out, described as "lovely sites that I admire, frequent or owe to. Includes affies." Those links lead to other personal subdomains on domains including {@chewables}, {@happy-mix}, and {@neonlove|neonlove.net}.

Most personal sites were built from basic HTML, a free host's page builder, or a drag-and-drop editor, then decorated with graphics and resources collected from other sites. {@geocities}' own drag-and-drop tool was {@pagebuilder}: "Create a cool, full-featured site without any HTML coding. Customize your pages with pictures, sounds, Add-Ons and more. Click 'Launch {@pagebuilder}' to start from scratch, or choose a pre-made template below."[@2001-pagebuilder]

Learning and Copying Code

Sites that taught HTML mostly did it by handing over code to copy. {link:external|https://www.funkychickens.com/main.asp}Funky Chickens HTML Help{/link}[@funky-chickens-html-help], created in {y:1999}, broke HTML into little effects you could copy straight into your page. {link:external|https://lissaexplains.com/lissa.shtml}Lissa Explains it All{/link} was an even earlier pioneer: a colorful, easy-to-follow HTML tutorial site launched in {y:1997} by 11-year-old {@lissa-brown} specifically to help kids and beginners build webpages without an automatic page builder. Her notes began on {@aol}, moved to {@geocities} as the site grew, and then moved to their own domain when she was 12. {dead:domain-repurposed|@cyp2|href=http://www.cyp2.com/|tip=Offline: this site no longer exists}CYP2 / ColorYourProfyle{/dead}[@cyp2-2004-archived] ({link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20040205122332/http://www.cyp2.com/}archived{/link}) did something similar for {@aol} profiles and webpages: color tables, moving and fading text, ALT characters, Wingdings and Webdings, font codes, premade profiles, webpage dolls, and whatever else could be squeezed into {@aol}'s tiny editable boxes. You didn't necessarily sit down to "learn web development." You learned enough HTML to change the background, copied a {tip:Scrolling text or images created with the non-standard HTML <marquee> element}marquee{/tip} from somewhere, found a color code somewhere else, broke the page, and eventually figured out why. A lot of that learning was just right-clicking someone's page and hitting "View Source" to see how they'd done something, then lifting the part you needed. That's also the actual reason so many pages ran disabled-right-click scripts: not a mystery gimmick, just an attempt to stop exactly that. It didn't always work, either as a deterrent or as a lock. At seven years old, I copied code straight off a site that hadn't bothered with one, and still got called out for it in my own guestbook.

{@neopets} built HTML editing into the site itself. Every Neopet came with its own species-specific petpage, a complete little website whose existing HTML could be edited, rearranged, decorated, or replaced entirely. {@neopets} allowed users to create editable pet homepages by {date:2000-01|January 2000}, and probably earlier: the earliest surviving official reference is a {date:2000-01-14|14 January 2000} update introducing new variables for people already designing their pets' homepages, and since {@neopets} didn't start archiving site updates until that month, the feature's actual launch date is unclear, so it might have been a launch-era feature from {date:1999-11|November} or {date:1999-12|December 1999} when the site itself went up.[@neopets-new-features-january-2000] {@neopets} also kept a {link:external|https://www.neopets.com/funimages.phtml}Fun Images{/link}[@free-neopets-images] library of animated graphics with the HTML already written underneath, ready to copy and paste into petpages, userlookups, shops, and guild pages. {@neopets} published its own official {link:external|https://www.neopets.com/help/html1.phtml}HTML Guide{/link}, released {date:2000-09-22|22 September 2000} as a step-by-step tutorial running from basic tags up through the tables the site's own pages were built on.[@the-neopets-html-guide][@new-features-september-2000] It was marked complete on {date:2000-10-05|5 October 2000}, when lessons on banners, tables, and NeoCircles, {@neopets}' own take on the webring, were added.[@new-features-october-2000] Introduced that {date:2000-07|July}, NeoCircles were a small HTML snippet that linked petpages, shops, and fan sites sharing a theme.[@new-features-july-2000] It was a useful way to learn: you did not always have to start with a blank page or understand the code before touching it. You could begin with something that already worked, change it, break it, fix it, and gradually figure out what everything did.

Plenty of {@neopets} users learned from other players instead. Fan sites taught HTML, CSS, and layouts to other players, and tutorials became part of the fan-site ecosystem: {@darkest-faerie-lair} taught guild layouts, shops, lookups, and sidebars, and its owner spun the general coding lessons off into {@spiders-web}, a beginner web-design site covering site planning, CSS, HTML, graphics, and layouts.[@darkest-faerie-lair][@spider-s-web-tutorials]

Layouts and Site Structure

Sites that put more effort into design often started in a graphics program. A common workflow was to mock up a complete design in {@photoshop} or {@fireworks}, cut it into smaller image {tip:Sections of a larger design exported as separate image files so they could be reassembled in a webpage}slices{/tip}, and reassemble those pieces as a webpage using HTML tables.[@creative-toolbox][@ics-media-2000-html] {@adobe-imageready}, bundled with {@photoshop}, could generate the slices and HTML automatically, while {@fireworks} was closely integrated with {@dreamweaver} for the same kind of work.[@fireworks-dreamweaver-nutshell][@graves-steve-2000-dreamweaver] Designers also had to work around the screen sizes their visitors were likely to use. Around {date:2000|2000} and {date:2001|2001}, 800 × 600 was a common baseline, although the page itself had to be narrower to leave room for the browser.[@photoshop-6-screen-resolution][@human-factors-screen-resolution]

Those techniques filtered into the hobbyist web, especially among people who started taking their sites more seriously. Premade {tip:Layouts whose main structure was positioned with HTML table rows and cells}table layouts{/tip}, {tip:Layouts that use one or more inline frames to load separate HTML documents into fixed areas of the main page}iframe layouts{/tip}, and later {tip:Layouts built primarily from HTML div elements positioned and styled with CSS instead of tables}DIV layouts{/tip} circulated as downloadable resources that could be edited and reused. Many were designed around one large photograph, illustration, celebrity, anime character, or other piece of artwork, with functional areas worked into the image for navigation and content. {tip:An image containing multiple invisible clickable regions, allowing different parts of one graphic to act as separate links}Image maps{/tip} and {tip:Images or links that change appearance when the pointer moves over them}rollover links{/tip} could turn parts of the artwork into navigation, while custom scrollbars and tiny text helped squeeze content into small boxes. {tip:An introductory page shown before the main website, often containing artwork, browser requirements, warnings, or an Enter link}Splash pages{/tip} asking you to "Enter" became another staple of design-heavy personal sites. People kept whole collections of layouts to download, edit, and replace whenever they wanted a new look.[@what-is-the-old-web]

Iframe layouts were especially flexible because each frame could load a separate HTML document without replacing the rest of the page. A common arrangement put navigation in a narrow frame on the left and changing content in a larger frame on the right, but there was no fixed formula. A site might have two or three columns, several stacked frames, a header or footer loaded in its own iframe, or multiple small content areas arranged around the artwork. By around {y:2002}, layouts like these had become increasingly common, along with image-map navigation, smaller text, and more deliberately separated sections of content. Compared with the more wandering pages of a few years earlier, they could feel cleaner and more organized at the time, even if some of them have aged terribly.[@iframes-amateur-web-design]

Three-column layouts could also take the form web designers came to call the {tip:A classic webpage structure with a full-width header and footer and three columns between them, usually a main content column between two sidebars}Holy Grail layout{/tip}: a header across the top, a footer across the bottom, and navigation, main content, and a second sidebar through the middle. The shape itself was simple, but reproducing it cleanly with CSS, especially with equal-height columns, fixed sidebars, a flexible center, and reliable behavior across browsers, was notoriously difficult.[@holy-grail-layout] Personal sites were rarely limited to one structure, though. Their layouts could be almost any arrangement that could be assembled from tables, frames, iframes, image maps, and eventually positioned DIVs.

Tables could do much more than hold a sliced page together. Designers used tiny transparent images such as spacer.gif to force table cells to particular widths and heights, and visual effects that would later become simple CSS often had to be assembled from image pieces.[@ics-media-2000-html] One common trick built a decorative frame from a 3×3 table: the actual text sat in the center cell while the eight cells around it held pieces of one image. The same basic technique could make a box stretch while keeping illustrated corners and edges intact.[@ics-media-2000-html] On personal sites, a block of content might appear to sit inside a glass jar, a pixel-art object, a ripped game sprite, or some other illustrated container. Designers sometimes distributed these pieces as resources for other people to build with.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary]

Within the more design-heavy personal-domain scene, especially among the girls and young women who often called themselves webmistresses, {tip:An early-2000s personal-site format divided into sections about the owner, resources for visitors, and information about the site itself}me/you/site{/tip} became a recognizable way to organize all of that content.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] The labels varied, but the split was familiar: me for autobiographical material, you for whatever was on offer to visitors, including adoptables, tutorials, and web graphics, and site for affiliates, cliques, fanlistings, credits, and an archive of previous layouts. Some people used me / you / www; others dressed the same three areas up as the girl / the goods / the www. A homepage might also carry a currently section listing what its owner was eating, watching, listening to, reading, or thinking about that week. Layouts changed constantly, and some people redesigned their entire site every few weeks because they'd learned a new technique or found a style they liked better.

Graphics, Websets, and Linkware

Tiny graphics made specifically for websites included tiled backgrounds, animated dividers, bullets, arrows, clipart, pixel sprites, matching Home / About / Links / Guestbook buttons, hit counters, email graphics, welcome signs, Online / Away / Updated graphics, and flashing {tip:A sign, often paired with a construction-worker or barricade animation, signaling that a website or section was still being worked on|tag=span}"Under Construction"{/tip} banners. Little "NEW!" icons pointed at whatever had changed. Background MIDI, then WAVs and MP3s, made the page audible too. "Best viewed in {@netscape}," "Best viewed at 800 × 600," browser badges, HTML-validation buttons, "Made with {@notepad}" graphics, and dated update logs were familiar fixtures.[@cameron-s-world][@88-31][@web-badge] A lot of pages also ran small scripts and HTML tricks: marquees, cursor trails, clocks, {tip:JavaScript that replaced or changed the text shown in a browser's status bar, often when hovering over a link}status-bar messages{/tip}, disabled right-clicks, rollover images, pop-ups, splash screens, browser-specific entrances. Personal sites rarely felt finished because people kept adding things to them, which probably explains why "Under Construction" survived for so long.

You did not have to make every graphic or piece of a site yourself. Large libraries existed specifically to supply {tip:People responsible for creating and maintaining websites}webmasters{/tip} with parts. {link:external|https://asd.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/ircatalog/cio/acknowl.html}Randy's Icon Bazaar{/link}[@nasa-goddard-cio-acknowledgments] distributed icons. {link:external|https://www.animationfactory.com/webgraphics.php}Animation Factory{/link}[@web-graphics] became a major source for the unmistakable rendered animated GIFs and 3D clipart that spread across personal sites. {dead:page-offline|href=http://www.grsites.com/archive/textures/|tip=Offline: this site no longer exists}{@grsites}' Absolute Background Textures Archive{/dead}[@grsites-absolute-background] ({link:archive-mirror|https://vlsicad.eecs.umich.edu/BK/Slots/cache/www.grsites.com/}mirror{/link}) offered thousands of seamless backgrounds alongside buttons, bullets, arrows, rules, icons, and generated headings.

Graphics creators put together coordinated websets: a matching background, divider bars, bullets, headers, navigation buttons, and usually a small button crediting whoever made the set. {@moyras-web-jewels} offered websets and other free graphics alongside hundreds of other sites distributing linkware, custom sets, and freebies.[@free-background-sets][@misc-sets-linkware-terms][@mirror-free-linkware][@matching-sets-for-web-pages]

{tip:Japanese for materials or raw materials; in web design, small graphics made for building and decorating websites}Sozai{/tip} (素材, literally "materials") were pixels made to do a job: dividers, bullets, navigation icons, Home and Back and Top and Email graphics, blank buttons for visitors to label themselves, and other small pieces for assembling a page, usually offered in coordinated colors. On me/you/site pages they lived in the you section with everything else laid out for visitors to take. Parts of today's revival scene use the word for almost any tiny sugary pixel graphic, but the older sense was functional.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary]

{tip:A free web resource distributed on the condition that its user link back to the creator}Linkware{/tip} was a form of free web graphic distributed on the condition that the person using it linked back to its creator.[@misc-sets-linkware-terms][@mirror-free-linkware] Creators set their own additional terms. They might require a credit button on the same page, forbid alterations, restrict commercial use, ask that a webset remain together, or prohibit redistribution. Permission to use a graphic did not include permission to repost it as part of your own collection. Tutorials and resource lists often directed visitors to the original site rather than re-uploading the files themselves.

The files were supposed to be saved and uploaded to your own server. "DO NOT DIRECT LINK" warnings were common because {tip:Embedding a file directly from somebody else's server instead of uploading a copy to your own}hotlinking{/tip} somebody else's GIF made their server deliver it whenever somebody loaded your page, using their bandwidth instead of yours. It was routinely called {tip:Common early-web term for hotlinking another site's files and making that server pay the bandwidth cost}bandwidth theft{/tip}. Site owners could block outside requests or replace a hotlinked file with a warning graphic, making the offending site display whatever image the original owner chose to serve. Some creators included blank buttons or matching fonts so a set could be extended without altering the original graphics; others had extremely specific rules about what could be recolored, modified, mixed, or redistributed.

Personal sites often combined backgrounds, fonts, dolls, scripts, brushes, buttons, and other pieces made by different people. Credits and Resources pages listed those sources and linked back to their creators. Attribution was inconsistent, links died, and graphics escaped their original context constantly, but crediting the creator and linking back were established parts of the resource culture.

Layouts were distributed the same way. Creators offered complete layouts for other people to use and customize: change the colors, replace the main image, rewrite the content, keep the bones. The creator's credit and link back generally stayed with the layout, and the creator decided how extensively its code and graphics could be changed. That permission applied to layouts offered as resources, not to any layout somebody happened to see on another person's site. Copying someone's personal design or code without permission, removing somebody else's credit, or presenting their work as your own violated the same rules that applied to graphics. Some creators allowed extensive modification; others were considerably more "DO NOT TAKE MY CODE" about it.

The Social Web Before Social Media

Personal sites had ways for visitors to interact long before social media feeds. Hit counters told you somebody had visited; guestbooks let them leave proof, usually with a name, website address, and sometimes a personalized {tip:A small graphic made specifically to leave in somebody else's guestbook}calling card{/tip}.[@zak-a-doodle-graphics-calling-cards][@archived-calling-cards-page]

By the early {date:2000|2000s}, {tip:Small embedded message boards or chat-like widgets that let visitors leave short public messages directly on a webpage}shoutboxes and tagboards{/tip} put a tiny public message stream directly on the page, somewhere between a guestbook and live chat. Services such as {@tagboard} were already turning up on personal sites by {y:2002}, with {@cbox} following a little later. Site News and Updates pages gave the owner a running place to note whatever had just been added, changed, or redesigned.

Cliques and fanlistings[@about-faq] overlapped enough that the distinction could get fuzzy. A fanlisting was, literally, a list of fans of one particular subject, and it did not require a website at all. Cliques were looser. They could be built around an interest, identity, joke, preference, design idea, or some tiny gimmick somebody thought would be fun to pass around, and many expected you to have a site to put the code on. That code did not have to be an image. A {tip:A web clique represented by styled text or a short text code instead of a graphic button or banner}text clique{/tip} might be little more than a word, a heart, and a particular font. {link:external|https://thefanlistings.org/}{@the-fanlistings-network}{/link} opened in {date:2000-05|May 2000} and became a central directory for fanlistings. A {@buffy-the-vampire-slayer} fanlisting badge still meant, more or less, "I like Buffy." A clique could say "DESIGN WHORE," "I ♥ MY COMPUTER," name your favorite font, assign your website a scent, or invent some other excuse to put one more strange little thing on your page.

Sites at the time drew the lines between these categories differently. {@pick-me} divided it in {y:2004} into cliques, webrings, listings, rotations, exchanges, and fanlistings, and those categories already overlapped while they were in use.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] In the more design-conscious personal-domain scene, plenty of cliques were far more selective than a copy-and-paste code: applications, rules, member lists, and joining graphics no more than about fifteen pixels high, with "elite" cliques making exclusivity the entire appeal. Some required applicants to own a domain or be hosted on somebody else's, and said outright that {@angelfire}, {@tripod}, {@geocities}, {@freeservers}, and the rest of the free hosts would not do.

Some cliques were about the website itself. {@scented-angels}, which former member {@kirryn} dates to {y:1999} and credits to {@mandy-clark}, who went by {@tira}, asked members to decide what their current layout would smell like based on its colors and design. A green-and-white layout might be mint, fresh-cut grass, green apple, or, if that was honestly where the design took you, broccoli. {@kirryn} remembers it at its peak around {y:2000} and {y:2001}, turning up especially around anime and manga personal sites even though the clique itself had nothing to do with anime. She started {link:external|https://aromatic.wings.nu/about.php}Aromatic{/link} in {y:2018} as a reboot rather than a memorial, and it runs as a working clique; her own word for it is reboot, not tribute.[@kirryn-about-aromatic-reboot-of]

Affiliates, sister or brother sites, and ordinary link exchanges described slightly different relationships between personal sites. An affiliate usually meant some kind of ongoing link exchange, often between sites with similar subjects or audiences. Sister and brother sites could imply a closer relationship between the owners, but the terminology was loose: some people used "sister site," "affiliate," and even {tip:A system where participating websites displayed one another's banner ads so their own banner would be shown on other member sites}"banner exchange"{/tip} for almost the same arrangement. A simple link exchange could be more impersonal still: you linked me, I linked you, and that was the whole relationship.

In the personal-domain scene, affiliates were often called affies. Two owners arranged it over email, then each displayed the other's little button, commonly around 32×32, on the homepage or in the section about the site itself. It was reciprocal and personal, closer to what would now be called mutuals than to a commercial affiliate program, and some webmasters were as selective about affies as they were about cliques.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary]

Webrings, around since {y:1994},[@webring-history] connected sites on a shared subject into a navigable chain. Previous, Next, Random, and similar links carried visitors from one member to another, so you could start on a {@sailor-moon} page, keep clicking Next, and eventually work your way through an entire ring of {@sailor-moon} sites. The ring was a way to actually move around the web, not just a badge of membership.

Some sites kept an entire {tip:A personal-site page used to display cliques, fanlistings, webrings, affiliates, campaigns, and other groups or networks the webmaster had joined}Joined page{/tip} for all of this: cliques and fanlistings they belonged to, webrings they'd joined, affiliates, sister sites, campaigns, and whatever other little networks they had accumulated. These pages could run to dozens of buttons and memberships. Read through one and you got a pretty good picture of where that person spent their time online, what they liked, and which other sites they considered part of their neighborhood.

Website awards put one webmaster's approval on another person's page, at least in theory. Some had applications, published criteria, and an actual review before you were allowed to display the graphic. Others were handed out casually, and some were simply offered for anyone to take and put on their own site. Those free awards were basically participation trophies, but they still served a purpose: the person taking one got something that looked like recognition, and the site that made it got its name and link onto somebody else's page. Contest-winner badges, challenge awards, and participation graphics all looked similar without necessarily meaning the same thing; sometimes you had won something, sometimes you had completed the challenge, and sometimes, with great distinction, you had shown up.

More selective awards often listed the winners, sometimes right on the homepage, which could send a little attention back to their sites. The association could matter in either direction. You might display an award because you admired the site that gave it to you, while someone giving an award to a site they admired could put that site's name and link on their own page and publicly associate themselves with it. Some people collected enough awards to need entire sections just to display them.

Between fanlistings, cliques, affiliates, sister sites, webrings, and awards, a personal site had many ways to describe a relationship: fan, member, ally, friend, endorsed.

Personal sites had no built-in likes, shares, or follower counts, but there were plenty of ways to get eyes on one: webrings and blog rings, affiliates and link exchanges, Link Me buttons, credit link-backs from anyone using your graphics, directories, awards, topsites, and banner exchanges. Guestbooks left another trail between sites; people could sign one and leave the address of their own homepage behind.

Topsites ranked member sites by visitor votes, so a "Vote for me" button asking people to click through and raise your rank was perfectly normal. Banner exchanges automated another version of the same traffic-sharing idea: you displayed banners from the exchange on your site and earned displays of your own banner elsewhere. All of this sat alongside the more personal links between sites, but the goal was simpler: get somebody to click.

Graphics to Collect, Trade, and Display

A lot of these tiny graphics worked as identity markers. An {tip:A tiny standardized web-button format commonly used to link to websites, advertise software or browsers, show affiliations, or display badges}88×31 button{/tip} could advertise a website, browser, cause, fandom, or affiliation. Later, {tip:Usually 99×56 postage-style graphics popularized on DeviantArt in 2005 and used to display interests, fandoms, opinions, and identity}stamps{/tip}, fanlisting buttons, {tip:Long, thin graphics, commonly 350×19, used in forum signatures to identify interests, fandoms, software, games, or affiliations}userbars{/tip}, sig tags, and blinkies could tell you what somebody liked before you'd read a word they'd written. People filled whole pages with them because collecting them was part of the site.

Buttons, Badges, and Blinkies

One of the most characteristic uses of an 88×31 button was pointing back to your own site. A Link Me button meant: save this image to your own server and use it to link to my homepage. Webmasters often offered several designs, and sometimes several sizes, so their buttons would fit neatly alongside whatever a linking site already used. A Link Me button represented the site itself, not an affiliation or a badge of identity. The same 88×31 format was also used for "Best viewed in {@netscape}" and "Best viewed in {@internet-explorer}" badges, software and hosting logos, and {@w3c|W3C} validation buttons.

The smaller 80×15 {tip:A tiny 80×15 web button format popularized in the early 2000s, especially in blog sidebars and rows of site badges}antipixel{/tip} or brilliant button appeared in late {y:2002} and spread quickly, especially through blogs. Its tiny size made it easy to stack whole rows of them together. They advertised browsers, operating systems, editors, web standards, RSS and Atom feeds, software, directories, hosts, and other sites, but they were just as useful for declaring interests, affiliations, preferences, or jokes. A row of them could end up reading like a miniature profile.

The most familiar blinkie was the narrow animated banner, usually around 150×20, with a border or other part of the graphic flashing between frames. Another was built from individual letter blocks, sometimes with tiny icons added, with the letters or blocks lighting up, changing color, bouncing, or moving in sequence. Surviving examples show the block style in use by {y:2003}, particularly around doll sites and later {@neopets} fansites, and tutorials describe blinkies as being assembled from separate "blocks, icons and letters."[@make-animate][@old-graphics][@graphics-info] The two forms continued alongside each other rather than one simply replacing the other. The block version is now often called a chain, block, or bead blinkie; {@glitter-graphics} still files them under Chain Blinkies.[@reddit-2026-question][@chain-blinkies] On {@neocities} and other old-web revival sites, "blinkie" more often means the 150×20 banner again, while 88×31 has settled into its own category as a website button.[@88-31][@web-revival-glossary] The name stayed the same even as the graphic changed.

On me/you/site pages blinkies ran smaller. An 88×15 format was common enough that two of them stacked matched the height of a standard 88×31 button, and thinner versions shrank to around 11 or even 6 pixels high, lettered in pixel fonts such as Bangalore and Redensek.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] A blinky, as one person put it in {y:2002}, was more or less the web equivalent of a bumper sticker.

{@binky-beads}, made by {@midgee} of {link:external|https://namaii.com/}Namaii{/link} in {y:2001}, turned plastic pony-bead bracelets into a tiny web-building kit. Each bead was a 10-pixel square, and the set ran through letters, animals, happy faces, leopard print, and a pile of colors, so you could string together names, words, and bracelet-like strips. People used them on splash pages, as navigation, or dropped straight into a line of text. The original {dead:page-offline|href=http://binkybeads.tk/|tip=Offline: this site no longer exists}binkybeads.tk{/dead} later moved to {link:external|https://namaii.com/binkybeads}Binky Beads{/link}, where the last update is listed as {y:2004} and the bead vault itself is no longer reachable.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary]

Adoptables and Trading

Adoptables were made to be collected. Artists offered related sets of animals, dolls, fantasy creatures, food, plants, toys, and almost anything else for visitors to adopt, save to their own server, and display on their website with a link back to the creator. Adoption worked as a link exchange as much as a giveaway. In some cases a design could be adopted freely by anybody who wanted it; in others a particular pixel went to one adopter only, handled through email or a web form.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] The pets themselves, also called cyberpets or virtual pets, might come with names, personalities, certificates, care instructions, or a tiny fictional habitat.

Some mystery eggs and hatchables added an actual surprise. Instead of choosing the finished creature, you adopted an egg and waited to see what it became. Some worked by having the adopter display an image from the artist's server so the artist could later replace the egg file with its hatched form; the URL stayed the same, but the graphic on your page changed. There was no guarantee what you would get. It was a tiny web-graphics {tip:Japanese capsule-toy vending machines where the item received is random}gachapon{/tip}.

Later, clickable adoptables turned the same idea into more of a game. You adopted an egg or pet and put its image and link in a forum signature, profile, or website; other people's views or clicks could help it hatch, grow, get fed, or level up. {link:external|https://dragcave.net/|@dragon-cave}Dragon Cave{/link}[@dragon-cave-dragon-cave], launched in {y:2006}, became one of the best-known examples, followed by sites and systems such as {@valenth}, {@squiby}, {link:external|https://magistream.com/|@magistream}MagiStream{/link}, and later {link:external|https://pokefarm.com/|@pokefarm}PokéFarm{/link}. Forum signatures could fill up with creatures waiting for clicks, which helped the pet grow while advertising the adoption site everywhere the owner posted.[@pokemon-adoption-center]

People also made graphics specifically for each other. Gifts, freebies, trades, personalized site titles, dolls, buttons, and signatures were part of the social life around personal sites. Someone might keep an entire page of graphics made for them by friends or visitors, with each one linked back to whoever made it.

Toyboxes, Pixel Clubs, and Cliques

All of those little acquisitions needed somewhere to go. A {tip:A page on a personal website used to display adopted or collected pixel art, usually with links back to the artists}toybox{/tip} was a page for displaying what you had adopted or been given: pets, food, charms, dolls, toys, gifts, patches, and whatever other tiny pixels had followed you home from somebody else's site, with links back to whoever had made them. Surviving {y:2003} examples include the adoption toybox on {@cathies-crazie-creations}.[@evastars-pfft-illustrated-glossary] The name was most at home in the personal-domain and hosted-subdomain pixel scene, though the collecting habit turned up all over. A toybox was a display case, not a club.

Some artists drew toybox pixels specifically for that kind of collection. They were free to take and display under the artist's rules, which did not automatically make them general-purpose web graphics. A pixel offered for somebody's toybox was not necessarily permission to recolor it, drop it into a layout, cut it up into a button, or build it into a collage. As with linkware, "free to use" could come with a surprisingly precise definition of use.

Pixel clubs made the collecting participatory. A club picked a theme, such as teacups, cats, quilt patches, cakes, charms, or lava lamps, and handed out a common template or base for members to work from. You made your own version, put it on your site, and then submitted it, traded it, or collected everybody else's, depending on how that club ran. Everyone started from the same little object; the point was seeing what each person did with it.

Teacups were one version of the format, remembered through the {@teahouse-mb} community and the current {@afternoon-tea} collection. {@kitty-friends}, which modern documentation credits to {@caz} in {y:2008}, used cats instead.

Pixel cliques overlapped with clubs but could be much looser. The shared part might be a size, a subject, a scrap of code, or a gimmick instead of one template everybody edited. Some worked mostly as affiliations; others asked members to make, exchange, or collect a small pixel object.

{@pocket-town}, created by {@lani} of {@neonlove} ({link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20080703053205/http://www.neonlove.net/lani/}archived original{/link}), turned a link exchange into a miniature pixel city. Members drew a tiny home or store, linked the building to a website, and arranged the buildings into their own town. {@lani} described it as "a cute little link exchange in the form of a city community." Buildings had to be between 20×20 and 60×60 pixels, small extras could go in the gaps, files had to be saved to your own server rather than hotlinked, and the main rule was simple: the building itself should always be a link. A modern {link:external|https://divergentrays.com/pocket}tribute at Divergent Rays{/link} keeps {@lani}'s introduction, rules, and templates and takes new buildings in the same format.[@pocket-town] I used {@neonlove} when I was younger, and this is one of the sites I remember from back then.

Other cliques turned the exchange into food and tiny merchandise. {@alexandra} of {@miss-behaved} remembers running {link:external|https://xandra.cc/history}Pixel Bakery{/link} in {y:2006} and {y:2007}, where members traded desserts and baked goods they had pixeled themselves.[@alexandra-the-museum-of-alexandra] {link:external|https://fairytrash.neocities.org/portal/yumyum}The Yum-Yum Shoppe{/link} used a storefront instead: members made their own 15×15 items, put them out for "sale," and collected one another's by displaying them in their own Shoppe with a link back to the owner.[@the-yum-yum-shoppe]

{dead:page-offline|@the-quilting-bee|href=http://www.theqbee.net/|tip=Offline: this original page is no longer available}The Quilting Bee{/dead} ({@qbee}) ({link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20071026081936/http://www.theqbee.net/}archived{/link}), founded in {y:2000} and active through {y:2014},[@theqbee-net-2007-quilting] was one of the more elaborate versions of the idea. Members made patches, 40×40 pixels each, traded them with one another, and "sewed" the patches they received onto pixel quilts displayed on their websites. The club also ran monthly pixel challenges, blog-a-thons, story writing, holiday-card exchanges, and other activities, with patches and pixel mushrooms given out for participating.

The {@neocities} user {@lostletters} kept a shrine remembering {@qbee|q*bee} from the inside, having been a member of both it and {@teahouse-mb} rather than writing about them only after the fact.[@lostletters-the-quilting]

Its founder, {@gurubii}, dated the club to {date:2000-02-18|18 February 2000}. {@jem}, who was already running the club by the {date:2007-10|October 2007} capture above and was still running it when {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20200812182722/https://theqbee.net/}a 20th-anniversary page{/link} went up in {y:2020}, preserved this account of where the idea came from:[@theqbee-net-2007-quilting]

It all started on Feb 18 {y:2000} when I was viewing a slander of me on someones site. i was reading in and when i got to the bottom it said "..I pity her, with shame and her quilt." I thought to myself "where does this quilt come into play?" then I was inspired to make a club to connect REAL friends and nice people all together by doing none other than building an internet quilt! this is a club, you join, get your name on the members list, and make new friends! each time you make a friend you exchange your patches and sew them onto your quilt! soon your quilt will be big and beautiful and colorful!

{@gurubii}, quoted on the club's About page

Membership was conditional and reviewed. An applicant needed a website of their own, a quilt page no more than three clicks from the front page, and a patch they had drawn themselves; the FAQ explicitly refused requests to make the applicant's patch for them.

A panel of members voted submissions up or down, and rejections were common: unoriginal graphics, a personal page with too little on it, text that broke in {@firefox}, or a missing official membership patch. {@geocities} sites were declined as a category, the club explained, not because of the ad banners but because firewalls and ad blockers left them loading as blank pages for many members. The selectivity was deliberate, and the staff said so plainly: admitting everyone would produce thousands of members and a club nobody could manage. Membership ran to roughly 350.

Upkeep fell to the members of the club. Quilts accumulated patches pointing at sites that had moved or disappeared, and pruning them was a recognized chore with a name, washing your quilt. Staff removed inactive members and dead sites from the member list for the same reason. The club changed hands several times across its long run, and most of the personal domains that hosted it along the way have since expired.

Online {tip:Trading card games run through websites, with image files used as collectible cards}TCGs{/tip} turned graphics trading into a game with rules. The community generally credits {link:external|https://tcg.fandom.com/wiki/Senshi_Card-Mania%21_R}Senshi Card-Mania!{/link}, opened {date:2002-01-22|January 22, 2002}, as the first fan-run online TCG.[@senshi-card-mania-r] Members earned randomized cards through games and site activities, traded duplicates with one another, and kept a {tip:A webpage where an online TCG player displayed cards they owned, cards they wanted, and cards available for trade}trade post{/tip} on their own website showing what they owned, what they were collecting, and what they had available. Your website was your binder. The hobby eventually produced layouts made specifically for trade posts and scripts for keeping increasingly large collections organized.

Dollz and Pixel Communities

Dolling shared many of the same sites, artists, adoption pages, contests, and trading habits, but one digital paper-doll lineage predates the web Dollz scene entirely. {tip:A Japanese digital paper-doll system in which separate clothing and image pieces could be dragged over a character in a dedicated viewer}KiSS{/tip} (Kisekae Set System) had been in development, according to the {link:external|https://www2s.biglobe.ne.jp/~yav/kiss/kissgse.html}KiSS/GS specification{/link} for {tip:A model in NEC's PC-9800 series of Japanese personal computers}NEC PC-9801VM2{/tip} computers since {date:1991-03|March 1991}.[@1991-kiss-gs] A KiSS set packaged a doll and its clothing into its own file format, which could then be opened in a dedicated viewer and dressed by dragging the pieces into place. It began in Japan and spread to other computer platforms during the {date:1990|1990s}, with later versions adding animation, sound, and other interactive features. It belongs to the same larger history of dressing little digital people, but technically it was its own system, with no connection to the graphical chat that web Dollz actually came out of.

The web culture that became known as Dollz grew out of {@the-palace}, the graphical chat system opened to the public in {y:1995}.[@the-palace-client-manual] {@the-palace|Palace} avatars were assembled from 44×44 graphics called props; a user could wear up to nine at once within a 132×132 area. Early Dollz often used three vertically stacked props in the middle of the grid for the body, leaving the others for clothing, and accessories. These could be layered onto the body in the {@the-palace|Palace} prop editor, drawn directly onto the {tip:A usually unclothed doll body used as the starting point for drawing or assembling hair, clothing, accessories, and poses}base{/tip}, or edited from a pre-existing clothed doll. Individual Palaces kept collections of saved avatars, often sorted into types like {tip:Community names for recognizable Dollz body styles}Preps, Sk8rs, Silents{/tip}, and whatever else that particular community had accumulated. Early names included "lil people" and paper dolls before "Dollz" became the name most widely associated with them on the web.

Once Dollz moved out of {@the-palace} and onto personal websites, they no longer had to work within the constraints of {@the-palace|Palace} avatars. {@geocities}, {@angelfire}, and independent domains filled up with finished dolls, downloadable bases, clothing, tutorials, contests, adoptions, requests, and drag-and-drop dollmakers. A dollmaker usually gave you a base surrounded by different hair, clothes, shoes, and accessories to layer onto it, after which the finished doll could be saved by screenshotting it for use on your own site. Additionally, people edited them, traded them, adopted them, entered them into contests, and made new pieces for one another. Crediting the artist who made the base or other parts was an important part of the culture. {dead:domain-repurposed|@dollz-mania|href=http://dollzmania.com/|tip=Offline: the original Dollz Mania is gone; this domain now hosts unrelated content}Dollz Mania{/dead} ({link:archive-mirror|https://dollzmaniabackup.neocities.org/|preservation}Dollz Mania Backup{/link}) and {dead:page-offline|@the-doll-palace|href=http://www.thedollpalace.com/|tip=Offline: this site no longer exists}The Doll Palace{/dead} ({link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20090422194012/http://www.thedollpalace.com/}archived{/link}) became two of the larger sites in a much wider network of personal Dollz sites.[@dollz-mania-dollz-mania-dollz][@the-doll-palace-archived-from]

By the early {date:2000|2000s}, dolling had also become a digital-art hobby of its own. Some artists drew original bases, clothing, hair, and poses themselves, either from scratch or on top of another artist's credited base. In parts of the community, dolls or pixel dolls became preferable to "Dollz" for this kind of work, partly to distinguish hand-drawn dolls from those assembled in a dollmaker. The terminology was never very tidy. "Dollz," "dolls," "pixel dolls," and "cartoon dollz" all turn up, and "dolls" alone is a terrible search term if what you actually want is a tiny pixel-illustrated person from somebody's {y:2003} homepage. More specific names often tell you more anyway: Preps, Sk8rs, Silents, Minis, Wonderkins, Divas, and plenty of others referred to recognizable body styles, poses, or base families.

What English-language doll sites later called Korean Dollz came from a different kind of system. Korean community and gaming sites were building commercial {tip:A graphical representation of a user or character in an online service}avatar{/tip} systems around the turn of the {date:2000|2000s}. {@sayclub} began offering avatars in {y:2000} and started selling clothing and accessories for them that {date:2000-11|November}; {@freechal}, {@cyworld}, and other services developed their own {tip:Systems where users acquired, and often purchased, virtual clothing and accessories to customize an online avatar}avatar economies{/tip} around the same period. The bodies, hairstyles, clothing, and accessories were professionally made for the service rather than drawn by hobbyists, and users customized an avatar from the pieces available to them, often buying individual items as virtual goods. {@cyworld}'s {tip:Cyworld's customizable avatar, displayed inside a user's minihompy and Miniroom}MiniMe{/tip} lived inside the user's {tip:Cyworld's personal profile/homepage space, combining social posts, customization, an avatar, and a decorated virtual room}minihompy{/tip} and decorated {tip:A small virtual room on Cyworld that users decorated and populated with their MiniMe avatar}miniroom{/tip}, so the avatar became part of both the user's appearance and the little space they built around it.

{dead:service-closed|href=http://candybar.co.kr/|tip=Offline: Candybar has shut down}{@candybar}{/dead}[@candybar-south] was one of the Korean avatar systems that became especially visible to Western doll sites. Developed by Neople, it opened in beta on {date:2001-12-21|21 December 2001} as an avatar and gaming portal, with games including Love Love Studio and Kung Kung Tta. Around {y:2004}, its artwork began circulating much more widely in English-language dolling through eLouai's unofficial {@candybar} Doll Maker.[@elouai-s-candybar-doll-maker-elouai] eLouai took the existing {@candybar} assets and turned them into a browser-based customization tool, letting users combine the bodies, faces, hair, clothes, shoes, accessories, pets, backgrounds, and other pieces that {@candybar}'s artists had already drawn.

{@candybar} built its avatars as a stack of transparent GIF layers rather than as a single pre-rendered image. The avatar area was fixed at 150 × 290 pixels and contained 32 image slots. Unused slots loaded a transparent placeholder, while clothing, hair, body parts, accessories, and other elements were swapped into specific positions in the stack. The layer structure also allowed a single item to be split across multiple depths, so one part could appear behind the avatar while another appeared in front. Hidden form fields stored which item occupied each layer, meaning the site maintained the avatar as structured data as well as a visual stack of images. Because every asset appears to have shared the same coordinate space, {@candybar} could assemble and update the character dynamically in the browser without generating a new flattened image after every change which allowed them to maintain its animated layers.

The basic idea was somewhat similar to {@the-palace}, where multiple props could be layered over a base doll, although {@candybar} used a much more rigid slot and coordinate system for assembling the avatar and was only limited to what the site offered for customization.

Dollers regularly drew new clothing, hair, bases, and accessories for one another, but {@candybar} users mostly worked with the existing {@candybar} library. The artwork was much denser and more detailed than most of the small hand-drawn Dollz circulating on personal sites, with carefully shaded clothes, tiny accessories, glossy highlights, and comparatively large heads. Reproducing that style well was difficult especially for amateurs, and making new {@candybar}-compatible pieces was never the main activity. The attraction was the enormous collection of professionally drawn parts that were already there to combine. People sometimes edited a finished avatar afterward, but they generally were not building a new {@candybar} wardrobe themselves.

Western sites still absorbed the finished avatars into Dollz culture, collecting them beside Preps, Silents, pixel dolls, and other styles. "Korean Dollz" seems to have been mainly an English-language collector term; the Korean services themselves generally called them avatars and the individual clothes and accessories avatar items. Similar graphics also came from services including {@cyworld}, {@sayclub}, {@netmarble}, {@hangame}, {@daum}, {@buddy-buddy}, and {@freechal}. Much of that material is harder to reconstruct now because the original avatar systems were tied to user accounts and pages the {@wayback-machine} archives could never fully reach.

Forums, Messaging, and Profiles

Forums, {@aim}, {@livejournal}, and later {@myspace} ran alongside personal domains for years. Website buttons became profile badges and stamps. Guestbooks and comments coexisted. Doll and pixel-art communities appeared in avatars and icons as easily as on a homepage. Graphics sites added {@photoshop} brushes, textures, and templates beside the older GIFs and websets, serving whichever platform you happened to be decorating.

Forums offered two main spots to customize: an avatar beside your username and a signature under your posts. They could match as a set or have nothing to do with each other. Graphics boards often bundled requests for both, with the same designers making avatars and signatures.

Avatar and icon sizes depended on the platform, and people kept accounts and icons on several platforms at once. Forum avatars followed whatever limits the board imposed. {tip:AIM's small profile image shown beside a user's screen name}{@aim} buddy icons{/tip} ran 48×48 or 50×50, {tip:MSN Messenger's profile image}{@msn-messenger} display pictures{/tip} were 96×96, and {tip:LiveJournal's term for the small profile images attached to posts and comments}{@livejournal} userpics{/tip} were 100×100. Dedicated sites often supplied all of it, collecting {@aim} icons, {@msn} pictures, {@yahoo} smileys, and away messages by the thousands.

Signatures were the customizable block automatically attached beneath forum posts. They could be one line of text or a quote, but people often treated the space like a miniature profile: custom graphics, usernames, favorite characters, animations, links, whatever the board's vertical space and content rules and limits allowed. Making signatures became its own branch of web graphics. You could make one yourself, ask a friend, or post in a request thread where a designer took your username, character or image, colors, text, and general direction, then posted the finished version back, sometimes with revisions or several options.

The graphic inside a forum signature was often called a sig tag. It usually centered on a character or other artwork, sometimes with a name or username worked into the design. People made them in programs such as {@paint-shop-pro} and {@photoshop} using swirl, grunge, sparkle, and vector brushes, along with pixel art, glitter, decorative type, and {@paint-shop-pro} tubes. You generally used one until you made or requested a new one to replace it.

{link:external|https://salvaged.nu/}Salvaged{/link}, maintained by {@belle}, archives brushes from older graphics programs, including the kinds of assets used to make graphics like these.

By the mid-{date:2000|2000s}, userbars were a forum-signature staple: long, thin 350×19 banners, usually with a tiny image on one side and text on the other, announcing whatever the bar was about. Because they were only 19 pixels tall, you could stack several of them inside a forum's signature-height limit. They were also much easier to collect and swap around than a full custom signature graphic.

Unlike most of these formats, stamps have a documented origin. {@deviantart}'s then-creative director {@ryan-ford} created the format for the {tip:A DeviantArt community event held in 2005 for which the site's original digital stamp graphics were created}{y:2005} {@deviantart} Summit{/tip}, originally planned for an airplane hangar; the little air-mail or postage-style badge made sense for the event. The two earliest recovered examples were posted on {date:2005-04-22|April 22, 2005}.[@ford-ryan-a-brief-history] The standard became a 99×56 graphic framed by that recognizable perforated postage-stamp border, either static or animated.

Users almost immediately turned stamps into tiny visual bumper stickers for their identities and opinions. They covered fandoms, {tip:Fan-supported romantic or interpersonal pairings between characters or public figures}ships{/tip}, software, music, games, political and social views, characters, aesthetics, jokes, and every variety of "I support…" or "Proud ___" declaration. Some had edgy Happy Bunny or Spencer's-tee energy; others were practical {@deviantart} etiquette, telling visitors to view the full artwork, not steal it, leave feedback, stop repeatedly viewing a page without {tip:Following a DeviantArt account so its new submissions and updates appear in your notifications/feed}watching the account{/tip}, or check whether requests and commissions were open.

The stamp was usually uploaded as a {@deviantart} {tip:DeviantArt's term for a work posted to the site}deviation{/tip} of its own. Other users didn't have to save and re-upload the GIF to display it. They could embed the original deviation with {tip:DeviantArt markup that embedded a thumbnail of another submission by using its numeric deviation ID}thumbnail syntax{/tip} such as :thumb427548925:, so the stamp on their profile still pointed back to the creator's submission.

{link:external|https://www.iconator.com/}Iconator{/link}[@iconator-buddy-icons] collected huge libraries of {@aim} buddy icons, avatars, wallpapers, and away messages. {link:external|https://www.glitter-graphics.com/}Glitter-Graphics.com{/link}[@terms-of-service] and {link:external|https://www.dazzlejunction.com/}{@dazzle-junction}{/link}[@dazzle-junction-free-graphics][@myspace-layouts] supplied glitter text, backgrounds, layouts, and copy-and-paste code for {@myspace} and other profile sites. {link:external|https://www.flamingtext.com/}FlamingText{/link}[@logo-design-and-name-generator] generated customized headings and animated text from whatever you typed into the box. The surface changed; the habit didn't. Forums, {@livejournal}, {@xanga}, {@piczo}, {@gaia-online|Gaia}, {@neopets}, {@myspace}, and {@aim} each gave people a slightly different place to put an avatar, a signature, a background, a profile layout, a comment, a badge, personalized text, or an identity graphic: the same handful of behaviors turning up again on a new surface every time users moved somewhere new.

You could take a background from one graphics site, a premade iframe layout from another teenager, learn how to change its scrollbar from {@funky-chickens}, grab an ALT character from {@cyp2}, add an official {@neopets} GIF with your referral link, put a {@candybar} doll beside the guestbook, and finish with a row of buttons linking back to everybody whose work you'd used. Of course it looked eclectic: half the page came from different people.

Website button ("Link Me") 88×31 Popularized by early web badges, including {@netscape}'s promotional buttons, and later recognized by the {tip:Interactive Advertising Bureau, an industry organization that standardized common web-ad dimensions; it later listed 88×31 as a "Micro Bar"}IAB{/tip} as the "Micro Bar." The exact dimensions were a convention rather than a browser limitation.
Antipixel button 80×15 Established by {@jeremy-hedley}'s Antipixel buttons in the early {date:2000|2000s}. The deliberately tiny format worked well for weblog sidebars and spread as a reusable template rather than because of a technical restriction.
Fanlisting code 50×50, 75×50, 100×35, 100×50 (sometimes 88×31 or 200×40) There was no single required size. Fanlistings offered several standardized code sizes so members could choose one that fit their own site's layout, and those dimensions became conventions across the community.
{@deviantart} stamp 99×56 Originated with {@deviantart}'s {y:2005} Summit promotion as a tiny digital stamp or bumper sticker. The original 99×56 template was copied for user-made stamps until the dimensions became part of the format; it was not a {@deviantart} image-size limit.
Userbar 350×19 Designed for forum signatures: wide enough for a label and small graphic, but short enough that several could be stacked without taking over a post. The exact 350×19 dimensions became a community template standard rather than a universal forum restriction.
Banner blinkie 150×20 The classic blinkie template was long enough for a short line of animated text while remaining tiny enough to stack with other graphics. The dimensions were a community convention, not a {@neocities} restriction.
Quilting Bee patch 40×40 A club standard rather than a technical one. A fixed square meant every member's patch sat the same size in everybody else's quilt, so a page of traded patches tiled into an even grid however many people you had swapped with.
Web banner (banner exchange) 468×60 Standardized as the web advertising "full banner," allowing the same ad to circulate through banner exchanges and unrelated websites without being resized. It was later formalized as an IAB standard ad size.
{@aim} buddy icon 48×48 or 50×50 A client-imposed small icon size that suited a compact chat interface, in line with contemporary desktop interface icons of the time. {@aol} does not appear to have documented why it chose exactly 48–50 pixels.
{@msn} display picture 96×96 Messenger displayed and resized profile pictures to 96×96 as part of its interface design. {@microsoft} does not appear to have documented why it chose exactly 96 pixels.
{@livejournal} userpic 100×100 A platform maximum suited to {@livejournal}'s text-heavy layout: large enough to work as an expressive image, but small enough to appear repeatedly beside posts and comments. The site's interface became heavily structured around the 100×100 limit, although the original reason for choosing exactly 100 pixels does not appear to be documented.
{@the-palace} prop / maximum avatar area 44×44 / 132×132 {@the-palace} was built around a fixed 44×44 sprite tile: the default roundhead and each wearable prop occupied one tile. An avatar could combine up to nine props across a 3×3 area, producing the 132×132 maximum. The reason 44 pixels specifically was chosen does not appear to be documented.

The End of the Personal Web

Between personal websites and standardized social media came a middle stage of customizable, centralized profiles, and {@myspace} is the important transitional case. Writing in {y:2007}, {@olia-lialina|Lialina} described people moving out of standalone homepages into "the language of {@myspace}" (accounts, profiles, journals, channels), while pointing out how much those pages still resembled their Web 1.0 predecessors: a background with blocks of graphics, text, and video laid over it, held together by the same modular, HTML-based aesthetic.[@lialina-olia-vernacular-web-2] {@myspace} moved identity and social interaction inside one company's service, but users could still make their page look unmistakably like theirs. In that sense, it extended the homepage habit rather than replacing it all at once.

Personal websites had always been social. They had guestbooks, affiliates, cliques, webrings, comments, message boards, and long networks of links between people. The bigger change was that the page itself became standardized. {@facebook}[@facebook] did not give users the same control over the appearance of their profiles, and that became increasingly normal. The broader shift called {tip:A label for the web's move toward interactive services, user-generated content, and participation inside online platforms}Web 2.0{/tip}[@web-2-0] put more user-created material inside centralized services, while the services decided how that material would be arranged and presented. Your profile could contain your photographs, writing, friends, interests, and updates without looking much different from anybody else's.

That made the standalone homepage feel increasingly old-fashioned. Maintaining one meant finding hosting, editing pages, uploading files, fixing broken links, and giving people a reason to visit an address they had to remember or bookmark. A social network already had the audience, the publishing tools, and everyone you knew sitting inside it. The trade was convenience for control, and over time fewer people needed a personal homepage badly enough to make one.

The old infrastructure lingered much longer than the culture around it. {@lycos} kept the remaining {@tripod} and {@angelfire} sites online long after the free-hosting era had passed, then took them down on {date:2026-04-24|24 April 2026}, ending nearly three decades of web hosting. The shutdown notice appeared on the {@lycos} homepage in {date:2026-03|March}, vanished while staying in the page source, and came back in {date:2026-04|April}.[@lycos-homepage-notice-to-our] The full chronology is on the {open:preservationModal}Preservation{/open} timeline.

What faded was a period when making a place for yourself online usually meant making the place itself: choosing where it lived, deciding how it looked, filling it with things you had made or collected, and linking it by hand to other people's places. Social media kept most of the people and much of the activity. It left less room for the page around them.

Glitter graphics outlived the homepage. How they moved on to profiles, to {@blingee}, and to messaging apps is in {open:historyModal}History{/open}.
