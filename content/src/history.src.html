History

{tip:Also called sparkly GIFs; Blingees are glitter graphics made with the Blingee editor}Glitter Graphics{/tip}, looping sparkle animations poured into text, illustrations, and photographs, came out of the decorative, homemade visual culture of the personal web: {tip:An aesthetic of excess, exaggeration, and deliberate ornamentation}maximalist{/tip}[@maximalism] {tip:Art and visual culture produced through the ordinary, informal use of digital tools and online spaces rather than primarily through professional art institutions}digital vernacular art{/tip}, excessive, sincere, highly reusable, and very far from the minimalism that took over mainstream web design. Most people associate them with {@myspace} profiles and {@blingee}; the tiles in this editor date from {y:2001}, five years before {@blingee} existed.

At least fifty-five of the seventy-four fills in this editor match surviving files from three sites built in {y:2001} by a small circle of {@webtv} users, {goto:HistoryGlitterConnection}the {@glitter-connection}{/goto}. The oldest dated tile is {@mica}'s, on a {@wtv-zone} server by {date:2001-06-07|7 June 2001},[@mica-2001-sparkbutton] and the pattern that dominates the collection is {goto:HistoryDanGemstones}{@dan}'s jewel-tone recoloring of it{/goto}. From there the tiles went into tutorials, onto {@geocities} pages and {@myspace} profiles, and into {@blingee}, and most of the makers' names {goto:HistoryLoss}got lost on the way{/goto}.

The personal-web culture glitter graphics grew out of has its own article, {open:personalWebModal}The Personal Web Aesthetic{/open}. For the broader preservation story and a chronology of the platforms that shaped the personal web, see {open:preservationModal}Preservation{/open}.

Origins and Early Development

Fill graphics existed before glitter did. A fill is a graphic meant to repeat, most often as a page background, and the same repeating images were also called tiles or bars. People had been making and sharing them since the early personal web, long before glitter became one of their most recognizable forms.

{@webtv} Networks was founded in {y:1995} and launched its service in {y:1996}: a set-top box, sold by {@sony} and {@philips} among others, that put the web and email on an ordinary television over a dial-up phone line. {@microsoft} bought the company in {y:1997} for about $425 million, later renamed it {@msn-tv}, and kept the service running until {date:2013-09-30|30 September 2013}.[@microsoft-1997-acquire][@2013-microsoft-pulling] A {@webtv} box was not a general-purpose computer. You could browse sites, send email, and build a homepage, but you could not install {@photoshop}, {@paint-shop-pro}, {@animation-shop}, {@adobe-imageready}, or any of the other desktop software graphics makers were using. So {@webtv} users built an elaborate graphics workflow out of browser tools, image processors running on somebody else's server, free webspace, and transloaders.

{@sparkelies} lists eighteen tiles by stone: Amethyst, Aquamarine, Blue Topaz, Emerald, Garnet, Gold-1, Gold-2, Peridot, Platinum, Rhodolite, Ruby, Opal, Sapphire, Silver, Tanzanite, Tourmaline, Topaz, and Turquoise.

{file:FB12.gif} is 61×29 pixels, with three frames lasting 110 milliseconds each, exactly matching the dimensions, frame count, and timing of {@dan}'s gemstone tile. The images themselves match too. When {@dan}'s tile is flipped vertically, the sparkles fall in the same positions as {@mica}'s, frame for frame. The main difference is the color: {@mica}'s tile uses soft shading with more than a hundred colors, while {@dan}'s version reduces the same pattern to five flat tones. {@dan}'s file also has a stretched row and column of pixels along its bottom and left edges. That distortion is already present in this version and carries through all seventeen tiles derived from the same pattern, rather than being introduced later by another graphics maker while recoloring or modifying the tile.

{@mica} is the earliest maker I can put a name to in this lineage, but the pattern that dominates this collection is {@dan}'s gemstone family, the branch that multiplied (the counts are in {goto:HistoryGlitterFileAnalysis}What the Files Reveal{/goto}). Nobody in that group is its single inventor.

What the Files Reveal

Most of the seventy-four sparkle fills bundled here are recolors of a handful of tiles. Everything below describes this collection, the files I was able to compare, not every glitter tile anybody ever made. Sixty-seven are flat tiles built from a few solid tones, and those sixty-seven reduce to about twenty distinct pixel patterns. One 61×29 tile accounts for seventeen of them on its own: {@dan}'s gemstone pattern, and eleven of his surviving gemstone files are byte-identical to fills in this editor.

Almost every flat fill in this collection follows the same recipe: two to five solid tones plus one pure white sparkle, with the white taking up twelve or thirteen percent of the pixels every single time, whatever the tile and whatever the color. Fifty-three of the flat fills use five tones in almost exactly the same proportions. The plainer ones are not a different idea; they are the same tiles with the shading knocked out. Flatten the four base tones of the orange fill into one solid color and keep the sparkle, and you have the black fill exactly, pixel for pixel. That is why black and light purple read as white glitter scattered over a flat background: that is precisely what they are.

The other seven fills, including the rainbow, camo, patriotic, light blue, pastel blue, and burn sienna tiles, are dithered instead of flat. They run from twelve colors up to two hundred and twenty-six, and they come from some other tradition of making these things.

Both sizes and all these variants are in the editor because I keep whichever surviving version I could actually find rather than quietly cleaning every old fill into one standardized copy. Most of them repeat a column down the left edge, some repeat the top row as well, and that doubled line shows wherever the tile starts over. It is part of what survives.

Making Glitter Graphics

Animation Inside Text

The earliest tutorial I've found for putting an animation inside text was from a page archived on {date:2001-09|September 2001}, and it was written for {@webtv}. {@vegasgal}'s {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20010906210443/http://www.angelfire.com/nv2/vegas_linda/names/animated.html#bottom}Animate Your Name{/link}, signed "Tutorial written by {@vegasgal|Vegasgal}©{y:2001}" and captured on {date:2001-09-06|6 September 2001}, walked {@webtv} users through putting an animation inside their name.[@vegasgal-2001-animate] You typed the name into a web-based banner generator, then took the result to {@magick-studio}, a browser front end for {@imagemagick}, and composited an animated bar into the letters. The animation in this capture is a swirling rainbow, not glitter. No desktop software was involved at all. {@webtv} users couldn't run {@paint-shop-pro}, so the editing happened on web servers and the finished GIF got {tip:Copied by a third-party server from an existing URL directly into your own web-hosting account, without saving the file to a local computer first}transloaded{/tip} to your own webspace. A transloader was another web service acting as a middleman: you gave it the URL of the file you wanted, plus the FTP address, account name, and password for your {@geocities}, {@tripod}, or other webspace, and its server fetched a copy and uploaded it straight into your account.[@download-upload] Where a computer user would download a GIF to a hard drive and upload it again, a {@webtv} user skipped the local computer entirely. Glitter tiles could drop straight into the same kind of workflow.

The Desktop Method

By {date:2002-01|January 2002} there was a desktop version of the method using {@paint-shop-pro}. {@bemymind}'s {link:external|http://www.bemymind.com/}How To Glitter Your Fonts Tutorial{/link}, dated {date:2002-01|January 2002}, survives on her own site, while an early repost on {@webwench} was captured by the {@wayback-machine} on {date:2002-03-27|27 March 2002}.[@bemymind-how-to-glitter-your] The tutorial opens by trying to work out something already circulating: "The most fascinating thing i ever saw, was the glitter 'Happy New Year' gifs that i saw these past few weeks." The example shown was "not made by me, would love to know who did make it!" A friend, {@ginger}, "located the websites that had the glitter gifs to share," and the tutorial sends readers to {@aylana}'s collection to choose one.

The method was straightforward: open a three-frame glitter GIF in {@animation-shop}, copy each frame into {@paint-shop-pro} and save it separately, flood-fill the text or dingbat once with each frame as a pattern, then reassemble the three finished images in {@animation-shop}. "If you have a glitter with more frames, you will fill your font/ding equal to that many frames." The glitter used in the tutorial, {file:sea-05.gif}, is 60×30 pixels with three frames at 110 milliseconds each, and is pixel for pixel identical to the dark green fill in this editor. That places the 60×30 family in circulation by the beginning of {y:2002}, already the standard material for filling letters and dingbats in a beginner's tutorial.

Blanks and Dingbats

A {tip:A prepared graphic used as the base, or matte, for a glitter fill or other applied effect.}blank{/tip} was a prepared graphic used as the base, or matte, for applying glitter or another fill, and almost anything could become one. Type a name in an ordinary font and turn the text itself into a blank; paint or draw a shape and use that; or take some other existing graphic and prepare selected areas of it, typically by making a color or range of colors transparent, so glitter could be composited in behind them. What made something a blank was its job in the workflow, not where the image came from, which is why the tutorials use the word for plain text graphics and pictorial ones alike.

Dingbat fonts were one source of shapes that could be turned into blanks, alongside ordinary text, painted shapes, and existing graphics. A {tip:A font whose characters are pictures, symbols, or ornaments rather than an ordinary alphabet; older dingbat fonts commonly mapped those images to positions normally used for letters and numbers.}dingbat font{/tip} is a font built from pictorial symbols, ornaments, silhouettes, or other small images sitting at the positions normally used for letters and numbers, so pressing a key could give you a shamrock, a star, a skull, a zodiac sign, whatever the font happened to map there. Wingdings, released in {y:1992}, and Webdings, released in {y:1997}, are two of the best known.[@microsoft-1992-wingdings][@microsoft-1997-webdings] Rendering one of those characters as a plain image and saving it was a fast way to get a blank without drawing a shape yourself, and a lot of surviving tutorials reach for dingbats specifically for that reason. The vocabulary stays loose here too: "dingbat" could mean the font, the character picked out of it, or the graphic rendered from that character. {@scri8e}'s Shamrocks page shows the dingbat case directly: "Most of the images from my Shamrocks index have been created by making a image from a php script. Then composited on dingbat Blanks, masks, models etc."[@shamrocks] {tip:An image used to control which parts of another image are visible.}Masks{/tip} controlled which parts of another image showed through; models isn't defined further here without more evidence of how the term was used.

The same site also kept a collection of downloadable dingbat and novelty TTF fonts behind a "No Peeking!" message and a reposted {y:2004} {@usenet} joke about "Fonters Disease," the particular frustration of a dingbat TTF whose useful symbols are hidden behind unpredictable keys[@ttf-ttf2], a reminder that fonts were as much a part of this toolkit as the finished tiles. {@webtv} made the blank-and-fill method especially practical, since its users had server-side tools and hosted assets instead of local art software, but the same idea carried straight into PC tutorials: prepare a blank from text, a dingbat, or some other shape, then fill or composite it, animate if needed, done in {@paint-shop-pro} and {@animation-shop} instead of a browser.

Image Fillers

{@stinkywetdog}'s {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20020809080323/http://www.wtv-zone.com/stinkywetdog/tutorials/3colorSparkleFont.html}How To Make A Three Color Sparkle Font{/link}, captured {date:2002-08-09|9 August 2002}, shows how elaborate the {@webtv} workflow could get: generate the name, transload it, bring it into {@imagemagick} with three animated sparkle bars, composite silver across the letters, red into the top, and blue into the bottom, add borders if you like, output a looping multi-frame GIF, and transload it home.[@stinkywetdog-how-to-make-a] The site's homepage was already offering visitors a way to "make your own custom fonts and frames" in a {date:2002-05|May 2002} capture.

An {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20020806161505/http://www.wtv-zone.com/scri8e/stars/flashites/index.html}{date:2002-08|August 2002} {@flashites} page{/link} calls hosted animated fills like these Image Fillers, "I.F.'s" for short, and explains how to let the animation show through only part of a picture, a shoulder or whatever the mask left transparent.[@moon-moondogee-flashlites-i-f-s] The page demonstrates with a {@hajime-sorayama} chrome robot illustration, a fifteen-frame animation with a filler sparkling across her arm and shoulder, whose own server timestamp reads {date:2001-07-13|13 July 2001}. Its disabled counter says it had been counting visitors since {date:2002-01-07|January 7, 2002}, though a counter's start date only says when the counter started, not necessarily when this page went up. Near the bottom, one asset is labeled "I.F. That Famous Darned sparkbutton!" with a marquee underneath telling visitors to "Note the date of file creation." The page never states that date, but {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20020806161505/http://www.wtv-zone.com/scri8e/bars/anim/sparkbutton.gif}the file itself{/link} is {@mica}'s tile, and its server copy was dated {date:2001-06-07|7 June 2001}. By {date:2002-08|August 2002} a fourteen-month-old GIF was already famous inside this circle. By {y:2004}, {@moon}'s methods overview listed seven different ways to glitter or sparkle part of an image: cutting out and overlaying; compositing ATOP without tiling; ATOP tiling across one piece, like a Prep doll's dress; traditional animation with sparkles in different places on each frame; table stacking; {@imagemagick}'s roll method; and "Morphimation," short for the Morph-Animated Method. The same page warns that compositing a still image into an animated glitter gives the finished graphic the glitter's frame count, so a 28-frame tile could push a picture past what an {@imagemagick} site would output.[@moondogee-2004-flashites]

From {@webtv} to the Wider Web

Early Finished Graphics

The finished graphics, meanwhile, spread across personal sites. Glitter graphics circulated through online forum {tip:A block of text or graphics automatically attached to the end of a user's forum posts}signatures{/tip}, and surviving examples turn up on {@geocities}[@geocities-yahoo-archived-from-the], {@angelfire}[@angelfire-lycos-archived-shut-down], and {@tripod}[@tripod-lycos-archived-shut-down] pages from the same period. Those three were part of a much larger personal-site ecosystem that had already given millions of people somewhere to publish their own pages, including {@aol-hometown}[@aol-hometown-aol-archived-from] and {@homestead}[@homestead]. {@geocities} in particular already had a fully formed visual culture years before glitter showed up on it. People made glitter graphics, collected them from other creators, reposted them, and wrote tutorials showing other people how to make their own, and some of that activity happened on hosts that had been around since the {date:1990|1990s}. The surviving captures don't tell me how common glitter was across {@geocities} as a whole, but by {y:2004} I stopped having much trouble finding it.

The clearest early finished example I've found on {@geocities} is a page called {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20030216061336/http://www.geocities.com/here2eternity1/h2e1/glitter.html}all that Glitters{/link}, captured on {date:2003-02-16|16 February 2003}. The page exists to show off the owner's own animated GIFs. "I am learning to make my own gifs from dingbats," it says. "This is a sampling of some of my work," and the page is a surviving example of the blank-and-fill workflow described above, made from dingbats specifically, not the place where that workflow starts. One graphic is credited to a friend, {@troy-mich}, who gave permission for it to be posted. Underneath the images is an instruction that turns up constantly in this culture: "PLEASE, download to your PC or transload to your own site as {@geocities} DOES NOT allow linking!" Here the warning invokes {@geocities}' own hosting rules rather than the creator's personal terms. The two options in that sentence map neatly onto the hardware split: computer users could download the GIF to a hard drive, while {@webtv} users could have a transloader copy it directly into their own webspace. The {date:2003-02|February 2003} capture is firm evidence that this page and its graphics existed by that date. A "© copyright {y:2002} {@here2eternity}" notice runs along the bottom, which tells you what the owner claimed for the site, not that these particular GIFs were finished in {y:2002}. It is no longer close to the beginning of this lineage. By then the {@glitter-connection} tiles had been circulating for about two years. The page earns its place because it shows a finished graphic and the fill that went into it side by side.

Whatever exact software steps {@here2eternity} used, the finished files show the same basic construction {@bemymind} had documented a year earlier: take a small tile of animated glitter and use it to fill letterforms or a shape. For the dingbat, the fill that went into it survives too. Its gold glitter carries the same eight-color palette and two of the three frames found in a 60×30 fill preserved in this editor's asset collection, run as a shorter two-frame loop rather than reproducing all three. So both halves of the process reach us in one example: the finished {date:2003-02|February 2003} graphic and glitter matching what was poured into it.

The dingbat uses only two of the tile's three frames. These animations were assembled by hand in {@adobe-imageready}, {@paint-shop-pro}, or {@animation-shop}, one frame at a time. Two alternating frames still read as a twinkling surface, and anybody turning out names, dingbats, and signatures in quantity had one less frame to place on every graphic. {@moon}'s {@flashites} pages make the same practical point: two different frames are enough to animate, and every frame in the glitter becomes a frame in the finished file. Whether that is why the third frame is absent here is guesswork; the maker never said.

Repositories and Rules

By {y:2004}, glitter repositories were posting their own rules for using their tiles. {@auburn-angel}'s {link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20040622040131/http://auburn_angel1.tripod.com/aagraphics/id16.html}Glitter Index{/link}, captured {date:2004-06|June 2004}, sorted original tiles into holiday, colored, diamond, water, metallic, silver, and gold glitters, allowed them on signatures, dolls, personal sites, borders, and non-commercial web graphics, and forbade redistribution, reposting as a collection, alteration, and hotlinking.[@auburn-angel-glitter-index-auburn] A {@geocities} glitter page by {@smhart}, with captures going back to {date:2004-06|June 2004}, put the bandwidth problem in one line: "Please do NOT link directly to the glitters. Glitter on Everything

Net artist {@olia-lialina} singled out another example while cataloguing the platform's visual conventions: a page in the {tip:One of GeoCities' themed neighborhoods. Early GeoCities sites were organized into named neighborhoods and sub-neighborhoods based loosely on subject matter}Heartland/Prairie neighborhood{/tip} that stamps itself "{link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20090830062810/http://geocities.com/Heartland/Prairie/1302/}Last Updated{/link}: Thursday, {date:2004-08-05|August 5, 2004}" and carries a Bible, a rose, and a layer of animated sparkle. She was struck by how out of place it felt: "Glitter is Myspace, not Geocities. Geocities are 90es, not 00s."[@lialina-olia-an-early-glitter] The graphic itself wasn't early: the surviving {@geocities} examples go back at least a year and a half further, and the tiles themselves more than three. Glitter was already circulating on {@geocities}, and she still read it as belonging to the newer social-web aesthetic that would become much more visible on {@myspace}.

By the mid-{date:2000|2000s}, whole websites existed just to make or hand out glitter graphics. Generators, signature makers, and graphics collections ran as destinations of their own, and the filenames, links, and source pages of surviving glitter assets point back to {link:external|https://www.glitterfy.com/}Glitterfy{/link}, {@glitteryourway}, {@123glitter}, {@glittermaker}, {@glitterkiss}, {@glitzheaven}, {@nicolshotel}, {@glittertextgraphics}, and {@cute-spot}, among others. Most of those domains are now parked or repurposed; the list of what survives is in {goto:HistoryGlitterSources}Resources{/goto}.

As glitter spread beyond the early {@webtv} circle, it was applied to almost anything: {@disney} and cartoon characters, celebrities and bands, dolls, anime, flowers, butterflies, angels, zodiac signs, fantasy art, photographs, holiday graphics, romantic messages, and a lot of hearts. Glitter graphics were also strongly associated with feminine web culture, particularly the highly decorated personal pages, profiles, dolls, friendship graphics, and romantic imagery common on sites used by girls and young women. Glitter also turned up in {tip:Youth subcultures that emerged during the early 2000s in the United States}emo and scene{/tip}[@emo], goth imagery, and what would later be described as {tip:A retrospective label coined by the Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute for a fashion and lifestyle aesthetic associated with the early 2000s}McBling{/tip}[@mcbling]. Many early examples share the same basic construction: a short sparkle animation, often reused in different colors, laid over text or an existing image. They served plenty of specific jobs too: a name or username spelled out in glitter, a "thanks for the add" graphic for a new {@myspace} friend, a birthday or holiday greeting, a romantic message, a comment left on someone else's profile, a forum signature, a profile picture, a personalized photo. Names in particular echo older {tip:A personalized graphic, usually made for use in a forum signature and often featuring a username, character, or decorative artwork}sig-tag{/tip} and custom-graphic culture: asking someone to make you a glitter name graphic wasn't so different from asking a forum designer for a sig tag.

Profiles and {@blingee}

When people moved from homepages onto profiles, glitter graphics moved with them: into {@neopets}[@neopets] {tip:Editable pages attached to individual pets that acted as mini personal websites within Neopets}petpages{/tip}, {tip:Customizable Neopets user profiles}userlookups{/tip}, and {tip:Neopets user groups with their own social and activity pages}guilds{/tip}, onto {@xanga} blogs and {@piczo} pages, and later across {@gaia-online}[@gaia-online] and {@myspace}[@myspace] profiles, anywhere a platform still let users set a background and paste in images.

{@blingee} arrived after years of people making, collecting, modifying, trading, and reusing small pieces of visual material online, the culture described in {open:personalWebModal}The Personal Web Aesthetic{/open}. {@blingee} pulled that process into one tool. Earlier, building a personal page out of reusable pieces meant assembling a whole website: a tiled background from one creator, buttons from another, a doll from somewhere else, a blinkie, some copied HTML, and your own text and photos. {@blingee} moved that same modular habit inside a single image. Instead of a website, you assembled a picture out of glitter animations, borders, text, stars, hearts, characters, decorative overlays, a photograph, and stamps other members had uploaded.

{@blingee}

{link:archive-wayback|https://web.archive.org/web/20091021203517/http://blingee.com/|tip=Web-based tool for creating layered glitter graphics with decorative stamps}{@blingee}{/link}[@blingee-archived-from-the-original] was a web platform for making glitter collages; individual creations were called Blingees. Launched in {y:2006} as part of the {tip:A network of teen-focused websites operated by Bauer Publishing}Bauer Teen Network{/tip}[@bauer-media-group], its browser-based editor let users layer decorative {tip:Blingee's term for reusable graphical elements that users could layer into a composition, including glitter, characters, borders, text, and other decorations}"stamps"{/tip} (from animated glitter and twinkling stars to complex image overlays) onto uploaded photographs. {@bauer} also owned the teen magazine {tip:A teen magazine first published in 1998; its name abbreviates the tagline 'Just for Teens'}J-14{/tip}, so it clearly understood the demographic.

In Japan, {tip:Japanese photo-sticker booths where users take and digitally decorate pictures}purikura{/tip}[@purikura] booths had been doing the same thing in arcades since the mid-{date:1990|1990s}: take a photo, then cover it in cute decorations, sparkles, and frames.

{@blingee} built a community around groups, contests, and ratings. It offered image dimensions optimized for {@myspace} profile pictures alongside built-in sharing to {@myspace}, {@hi5}[@hi5], and {@piczo}[@piczo]. Its export sizes and sharing buttons were designed around the profile sites where its users were already posting and talking to each other.

A {@blingee} stamp worked on the same basic idea as older web graphics: one person uploads something, and other people use it in their own work. Earlier creators set their own terms for that kind of reuse: credit required, no alterations, no redistribution outside the original webset. {@blingee} handled it through the platform itself instead. Once a user uploaded a stamp, any other member could use it in their own Blingees, and once somebody actually had, the uploader could no longer delete it.[@sousa-ana-matilde-blingee-hetamoe][@lialina-olia-oral-history-irina] Users built up personal collections of stamps this way, and those collections became parts of other members' Blingees whether or not the original uploader kept using the site.

In {date:2015-09|September 2015}, {@olia-lialina|Lialina} {link:external|https://blog.geocities.institute/archives/5348}interviewed {@irina-kuleshova}{/link}, an orthodontist in Rostov-on-Don, in southern Russia, who made Blingees under the username {@ivk} and had uploaded 4,559 stamps of her own.[@lialina-olia-oral-history-irina] She learned animation from downloaded tutorials and an unlicensed copy of {@photoshop} somebody gave her, and she worked out how other people's stamps were built by making a {@blingee} with one, saving the result, and pulling the file apart in {@photoshop}, because that was the only way to get a stamp off the site. A snow stamp took her two years of giving up and coming back to it. {@blingee} counted how often each stamp was used and handed out badges at a hundred, a thousand, ten thousand, and a hundred thousand uses, which is part of why copied stamps stung so much: whoever re-uploaded yours collected the points for it. "I am a free woman now," she told {@olia-lialina|Lialina}. "I married {@blingee}."

{@blingee} users made graphics in almost every style. The same editor could turn out a name spelled in glitter and hearts, a "Happy Birthday" card built from roses and animated text, an emo collage of skulls and broken hearts, a devotional image of Jesus surrounded by doves, or a fan edit of {@avril-lavigne}, and none of them would have looked out of place browsing the site's galleries. A huge share of what people made was personal and social: usernames and first names decorated with stars, flowers, hearts, or a favorite cartoon character, birthday and Christmas cards, good-morning and good-night graphics, and "I love you" or "best friends forever" pieces built around a bouquet, a teddy bear, or a coffee cup with a heart floating over the steam.

Blingees also reflected whatever subcultures and fandoms their makers belonged to on {@myspace} and similar sites. Scene and emo Blingees ran on favorite bands, skulls, broken hearts, and dark illustrations laid over a black-and-pink profile piece. McBling compositions loaded the same tools with rhinestones, metallics, designer handbags, flip phones, and celebrity faces. Hip-hop graphics wrapped a rapper's photo in money, jewelry, and flames. Fandom cut across all of it: anime and manga characters were common enough to form a visual culture of their own, and just as often bled into emo, scene, kawaii, or gothic treatments of the same image. {@britney-spears}, {@avril-lavigne}, {@justin-bieber}, {@selena-gomez}, {@miley-cyrus}, and the {@jonas-brothers} all turn up in surviving Blingees, alongside {@disney-channel}, {@icarly}, {@high-school-musical}, {@twilight}, {@barbie}, and {@hello-kitty}.

Fantasy Blingees put fairies, pixies, elves, and unicorns through the same glitter treatment as darker gothic work built from vampires, witches, and haunted forests. Religious users made their own devotional graphics: Jesus, saints, popes, crosses, and white doves layered with flowers and animated sparkle exactly like a cartoon character's dialogue bubble. Patriotic graphics did the same with flags, eagles, heraldic symbols, and national colors, and not only for the United States. A grandmother's birthday greeting, a glittering Jesus, and an emo broken-heart collage were all made with the same stamps and effects, and there's no reason to read any of them as a joke.

The platform nearly shut down in {date:2015-08|August 2015}. User outcry followed, new funding kept it online, and previously premium features became free. {@blingee} finally closed in {date:2024-06|June 2024}. The surviving closure notice did not give a reason, so attributing the shutdown to one specific cause would be guesswork.

Afterlife

As the window for decorating profiles narrowed and major free hosts closed or moved to paid plans, glitter graphics migrated instead of disappearing. They persisted across {@myspace}, {@tumblr}[@tumblr], {@facebook}, and {@whatsapp}, where image-based greetings became a daily ritual for many users. Circulated globally and often multilingual, people send them to say good morning, mark birthdays and holidays, send religious greetings, tell somebody they're thinking of them, and generally keep in touch with friends and relatives. They pair coffee cups, flowers, and decorative typography with animated glitter and sparkles.

Today Blingees resurface in nostalgic internet communities and on hosts such as {link:external|https://neocities.org/|tip=A free web-hosting service launched in 2013 and modeled partly on GeoCities}{@neocities}{/link}[@neocities], where people deliberately revive early web aesthetics. Glitter graphics also survive in searchable GIF libraries such as {@tenor}[@tenor] and {@giphy}[@giphy]. When {@google} closed {@tenor}'s public API in {date:2026-06|June 2026}, some third-party apps moved to {@giphy} or newer services such as {@klipy}.

The look has made its way into contemporary art and pop culture, especially {tip:Art that uses the internet as a medium or subject}Net Art{/tip}[@internet-art] and work that deliberately revives early web aesthetics. The video for {@mia}[@m-i-a]'s {y:2010} single {tip:A single from the album /\/\ /\ Y /\}XXXO{/tip}[@xxxo] draws heavily on the visual language associated with {@myspace}-era glitter graphics and {@blingee}, through glittering floral graphics and decorative stamps.

Glitter graphics sit comfortably inside both {tip:An aesthetic sensibility that finds value in exaggeration, artifice, and theatricality, often embracing what high culture dismisses}camp{/tip}[@sontag-susan-1964-notes-on] and {tip:Art or design characterized by sentimental, popular appeal, and mass accessibility, often dismissed by elite taste but embraced in everyday culture}kitsch{/tip}[@kitsch], the two words critics reach for when something is sincere and far too much at the same time. Both are useful for analyzing the work afterward, but neither is necessary to explain why somebody made one. Nobody needed a theory of camp to pour gold glitter into a dingbat. Most people were doing it because they liked how it looked.

What Got Lost

Screenshooting a graphic instead of saving the original file immediately turns it into a static image. A screenshot may catch phone or app UI at the edges, and each crop eats farther into the graphic. Repeated downloads and re-uploads add compression artifacts until something that began crisp and animated ends up blurry, pixelated, and still.

Even when a page survives, its externally hosted images often don't. Limited storage and bandwidth led many site owners to keep their images on hosts such as {@boomspeed},[@2006-boomspeed] {@tinypic},[@tinypic-archived-from-the-original] and {@photobucket},[@photobucket] and {open:preservationModal}those hosts shut down or put old embeds behind watermarks{/open}.

{@wtv-zone}, the host where much of this story lived, described itself as "wholly owned and operated by the husband and wife team of Larry and Kari Bumeder and our son Kyle," running its own webservers out of the Pocono Mountains since {date:2000-03|March 2000}.[@welcome-to-the-wtv-zone] A cascading hardware failure took the server down on {date:2022-11-18|18 November 2022}, "damaged beyond repair," and the closure, after "almost 22 years," went out ten days later in an email to subscribers, later shared by {@mona-young} in the {@friends-of-webtv-and-msntv} {@facebook} group.[@wtv-zone-2022-almost] The {@wtv-zone|wtv-zone.com} domain itself kept resolving under different, unrelated ownership for a while after that, and finally stopped answering in {date:2026-06|June 2026}.[@welcome-to-the-wtv-zone]

{@olia-lialina|Lialina} made a point about the wider early web that applies here: collecting old MIDI files and animated GIFs preserves the artifacts, but not necessarily what problems they solved, how people actually used them, or which communities they belonged to.[@lialina-olia-a-vernacular-web] That's part of why {open:personalWebModal}The Personal Web Aesthetic{/open} exists alongside this page: linkware, dolling, websets, fanlistings, adoptables, signatures, and {@myspace} layouts are what a glitter graphic was made to sit among, and the images tend to survive better than the practices that surrounded them.

The makers' names travelled worse than their files. In this editor, the seventeen recolors of {@mica}'s pattern go by {@dan}'s stone names, amethyst, ruby, and sapphire, and her own homepage survives only as a one-line greeting signed with a username, {@stardrops}. The tile spread much further than either name did.

How Glitter GIFs Worked

The {tip:Graphics Interchange Format}GIF{/tip}[@compuserve-1990-graphics] format, introduced in {y:1987} by {@compuserve}[@compuserve], could store multiple images from the beginning. The {y:1989} revision added timing and {tip:Instructions that tell a GIF decoder what to do with the previous frame before drawing the next one, such as keeping it, clearing it, or restoring an earlier state}disposal controls{/tip} that made browser animation practical. Combined with broad browser support, those features made GIF the foundation of animated glitter graphics. The spec stores that timing per frame, in hundredths of a second, which is why glitter speeds land on oddly specific numbers. {@steve-wilhite}[@steve-wilhite] insisted the name is pronounced like {tip:The American peanut-butter brand|tag=span}Jif{/tip}, sparking one of the internet's most heated debates. I pronounce GIF with a hard "G" and locked in that pronunciation by age six, so he should've spoken up sooner. May he rest in peace though.

On the desktop, glitter was assembled in {tip:Adobe's web-graphics editor, bundled with Photoshop, with tools for image slicing, optimization, animation, and GIF export}{@adobe} {@adobe-imageready}{/tip}[@adobe-imageready] or {tip:An image editor developed by Jasc and later acquired by Corel}Jasc {@paint-shop-pro}{/tip}[@paintshop-pro] with its companion GIF program, {@animation-shop}[@animation-shop] (the step-by-step methods are in {goto:HistoryMakingGlitter}Making Glitter Graphics{/goto}). Later, {@blingee}, {link:external|https://www.picmix.com/|@picmix|tip=A web-based tool for making glitter graphics and photo collages}PicMix{/link}[@picmix], and {link:external|https://www.online-image-editor.com/|@online-image-editor|tip=A web-based image editor with GIF animation tools}Online Image Editor{/link}[@online-image-editor] let people make similar graphics in a browser. {@picmix} and {@online-image-editor} remain available; {@blingee} closed in {y:2024}. The surviving tools felt too clunky and outdated for me to enjoy, which is why I built Glitter Graphics. No shade.

Most glitter fills run at nearly the same speed. Sixty-two of the seventy-four sparkle fills bundled here hold each frame for 110 milliseconds, roughly nine frames a second, and four more run at 100. That is slow for animation, and it has to be. Three frames at that speed read as a surface catching the light; run the same three frames much faster and the glitter stops twinkling and starts to crawl.

Seven of the fills ask for no delay at all, a plain zero in the timing field. Browsers have never taken that literally. Anything under two hundredths of a second gets clamped, historically to 100 milliseconds, so those files play at about the same speed as everything else no matter what they request. Whether the zero meant "as fast as possible" or was just a field nobody filled in, the browser decided for them. One outlier, a dithered pastel blue tile, asks for 20 milliseconds across five frames and actually gets it.
